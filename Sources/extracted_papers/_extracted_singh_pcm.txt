ABSTRACT
Solar water heaters (SWHs) are widely utilised to meet domestic and industrial hot water demands due to their 
technical feasibility and economic viability. However, their performance is significantly affected by the inter -
mittent nature of solar radiation, limiting energy availability to daylight hours. To address this limitation and 
ensure round-the-clock hot water supply, integrating Phase Change Materials (PCMs) offers a promising solution 
by enabling efficient thermal energy storage. This comprehensive review explores the fundamentals and clas -
sifications of PCMs, various integration strategies within SWH systems, and advanced techniques aimed at 
improving their thermal performance. Special emphasis is given to the use of nano-enhanced and hybrid PCMs, 
which demonstrate superior thermal properties compared to conventional materials. The review further exam -
ines experimental and numerical studies comparing different PCMs and system configurations, highlighting 
performance improvements through the use of nanoparticles, metallic foams, expanded graphite, boron nitride, 
and finned or encapsulated structures. It is observed that materials such as paraffin wax, fatty acids, sugar al -
cohols, and hydrated salts are commonly employed due to their suitable phase transition temperatures and 
thermal characteristics. The article also evaluates the economic feasibility, technical challenges, and future di -
rections of PCM-integrated SWH systems. Selection of appropriate PCM is found to depend on factors like thermal 
conductivity, charging/discharging rates, operating temperature, and required storage capacity. Overall, the 
findings indicate that PCMs especially in nano-enhanced or composite forms hold substantial potential to 
enhance the efficiency, reliability, and sustainability of solar water heating technologies.

Solar thermal systems equipped with thermal energy storage (TES)

plications. The energy storage improves the performance of solar de -
vices. Several distinct strategies for the storage of energy have been 
developed and currently in widespread use [ 11 ]. Sensible heat storage, 
latent heat (or phase change) storage, and chemical heat storage are the 
three main categories into which thermal energy storage technologies 
are typically divided according to their thermophysical characteristics, 
as shown in Fig. 1 [ 12 , 13 ]. Sensible heat storage, which uses tempera -
ture changes in solids or liquids to store energy, and latent heat storage, 
which uses material phase transitions (such as solid – liquid, liquid -
– vapor, or solid – solid) to store and release energy at almost constant 
temperatures, are further subcategories of TES in the context of 
non-reactive storage media. However, chemical heat storage offers a 
viable path for high energy density and long-duration storage since it 
uses reversible endothermic and exothermic reactions in reactive ma -
terials. A fundamental understanding of TES technologies is given by 
this classification framework. Of these, latent heat storage employing 
phase change materials (PCMs) is becoming more and more acknowl -
edged for its exceptional energy density and capacity to stabilize tem -
perature in solar water heating systems.
Solar energy is a time-dependent renewable source of energy that is 
inexhaustible and eco-friendly. Among the most often used applications 
of solar energy is for thermal purposes. On the other hand, thermal 
energy storage is an efficient way to combat the sporadic and diffuse 
character of solar energy [ 9 ]. Techniques for energy storage can work 
towards closing the gap between supply and demand for energy. Among 
the most feasible methods for storing solar energy involves the utiliza -
tion of specific organic and inorganic substances, which are referred to 
as phase change materials (PCMs), which enable the latent heat of fusion 
to be harnessed [ 14 ]. To improve the thermal performance of solar 
heating systems, PCMs can be used as an effective tool. PCMs can 
effectively store additional thermal energy during the day through 
fusion and release it at sunset via solidification at a consistent temper -
ature, which leads to a higher storage efficiency. PCM has a number of 
benefits. Specifically, its high heat storage capabilities compared to 
other heat storage materials lead to a decrease in the size of the thermal 
storage facility [ 15 ]. The isothermal phase change feature of PCMs, 
wherein heat is charged and discharged at a virtually fixed temperature 
(phase change temperature) matching the phase transition, seems to be 
another significant benefit. As a consequence, they are perfect for ap -
plications involving space heating where thermal comfort can be 
attained by reducing temperature swings. However, PCMs have a 
number of demerits such as PCM systems necessitating a long opera -
tional time to recuperate the installation cost. Conventional PCMs also 
have other drawbacks such as low thermal stability, thermal cycling, 
and low thermal conductivity [ 16 ]. There has been a number of attempts 
to review the implementation of PCMs in different solar energy systems,

2. PCM as solar TES
Solar energy is a pristine and environmentally friendly source of 
energy that has the potential to address the limitations and environ -
mental concerns associated with traditional energy sources. However, 
the utilization of solar energy necessitates the implementation of energy 
storage devices to store and then provide the energy for future usage. 
The implementation of a solar thermal storage system has the potential 
to enhance the performance of solar thermal applications by effectively 
managing fluctuations in solar radiation that occur over the course of a 
day. PCMs are considered to be the suitable material for TES because of 
their significant latent heat capacity. PCMs has the potential to store a 
greater amount of heat per unit capacity compared to traditional storage 
methods, because of their latent heat storage mechanism [ 44 ]. One of 
the efficient method for increasing TES is the utilization of PCMs [ 18 ]. A 
study discussed that PCMs absorb solar radiation and store it as latent 
heat. The heat storage of PCM occurs appropriately at constant tem -
perature as illustrated in Fig. 4 (a) [ 45 , 46 ]. Moreover, heat storage in 
PCM and its utilization for any purpose is a cyclic process. The cyclic 
process of the heat energy storage concept of PCM for solar thermal 
application is shown in Fig. 4 (b). As per practical applications, the PCM 
improves the efficiency of Rankine cycle. The PCMs are also widely used 
in low temperature TES systems, like building space heating, hot water 
distribution, and heat sinks for electronic equipment such as laptop 
computers [ 47 ]. PCMs provide an appealing solution to solar thermal 
storage; nonetheless, it is vital to thoroughly evaluate their long-term 
performance, cost-effectiveness, and integration techniques.
2.1. PCM fundamentals and classification
The use of PCMs has garnered significant interest among academics 
globally, primarily owing to its considerable potential in the field of TES. 
The broad temperature range capability and compositional adaptability 
PCMs render them highly suitable for a diverse variety of thermal 
storage and management systems. To develop a comprehensive PCM 
with exceptional energy storage capabilities and related properties, it is 
crucial to conduct a thorough comprehension of PCMs and their classi -
fication [ 49 ]. The detailed classification of PCMs are illustrated in Fig. 5
(a). Hydrocarbons, such as paraffin, fatty alcohols, and waxes, are used 
to make organic PCM. An inorganic PCM contains molten salts, salt 
hydrates, and metallic. Organic-inorganic, inorganic-inorganic, and 
organic-organic molecules are all included in eutectic PCM. PCMs can be 
used in numerous ways, such as particles distributed in bulk materials, 
matrices and sheets [ 50 – 52 ]. As a result of their excellent thermal 
specifications, organic solid – liquid PCMs are an important group of 
PCM, with a high TES density, a tiny temperature range of phase change, 
no or little super-cooling and congruent melting. The use of these PCMs, 
on the other hand, is limited by leak from the storage tank and of having 
less thermal conductivity [ 53 ]. Organic PCMs have twice of per unit 
volume latent heat storage capacity, greater heat conductivity and less 
expensive [ 47 , 54 ]. Inorganic PCMs, on the other hand, have been 
claimed to exhibit inherent flaws such as incongruent melting, corro -
siveness, super cooling and phase segregation characteristics [ 54 ]. The 
fundamental advantage of eutectic mixtures has been discovered to be 
their phase transition temperature, which can be controlled by mingling 
the several mass ratios of the elements [ 55 ].
In the specified temperature range, solid-solid PCMs maintain their 
bulk solid characteristics. They don ’ t have the leaking problems that 
solid-liquid PCMs do, and as a result, non-encapsulated solid-solid PCMs 
could be employed with different materials [ 58 ]. Due to the substantial 
variations in volume and pressure during change of phase, it has been 
stated that the use of PCMs in solid – gas and liquid – gas forms is limited 
[ 59 ]. Furthermore, PCMs can be classified as low (less than 220

2.2. Desirable properties of PCMs for SWH
The various categories of PCMs have their own advantages and dis -
advantages. Organic PCMs have garnered attention because of their 
adequate heat storage density, broad temperature operating range, 
stability over time, noncorrosive, and low toxicity features [ 65 , 66 ]. On 
the other hand, the organic PCMs that are often used in solar thermal 
conversions have number of drawbacks, the most frequent of which are 
naturally poor thermal conductivity, lack of fire safety, and a high price 
[ 67 , 68 ]. Inorganic salt hydrates have high phase transition enthalpy, are 
nonflammable, and cost-effective, making them suitable for TES appli -
cations. However, these salts often have severe supercooling, phase 
segregation, and corrosivity problems [ 69 ]. The effective properties of 
various PCMs as per advantages and disadvantages for solar TES are 
depicted in Fig. 5 (b) [ 57 ]. Further, PCMs used in solar-powered thermal 
applications should possess distinct desirable characteristics to maxi -
mise TES efficiency. The desirable PCMs properties are shown in 
Table .1 , and thermal properties like melting point and latent heat 
storage capacity is presented in Table .2 .
However, to select an acceptable PCM, the following parameters 
should be used in the following order: 
a) Latent heat of the material
b) Material thermal conductivity
c) Melting point of the material
d) Specific heat of the material
e) Density of the material
To ensure higher rate of energy storage and discharge, PCM must 
have a higher value of thermal conductivity, latent heat and a lower 
value of melting point. In case of imperfect contact between PCM layer 
and absorber material, the vacant spaces in the PCM layer will produce 
thermal contact resistance, lowering heat transmission between the 
layer of PCM and absorber material [ 78 ]. The lower cost of storage 
material lowers both capital and operating costs.
3. Integration strategies and enhancement methods of PCMs in 
SWH
The integration of PCMs into SWH systems has emerged as a prom -
ising approach to enhance thermal energy storage efficiency and extend 
hot water availability beyond sunlight hours. Recent advancements 
have focused on strategic PCM placement and encapsulation techniques 
to optimize heat transfer and thermal regulation. Researchers have 
explored several configurations, including direct immersion of encap -
sulated PCMs in the storage tank, incorporation within absorber plates 
of flat-plate collectors, and integration into thermosyphon systems. 
Among these, encapsulated PCM systems using spherical, cylindrical, or 
flat panel geometries demonstrate improved thermal conductivity and 
phase stability, especially when enhanced with fins, metal foams, or 
high-conductivity nanoparticles. Shell-and-tube and finned-tube ar -

efficient heat exchange characteristics. Moreover, cascaded PCM sys -
tems, which utilize multiple PCMs with varying melting points, offer 
superior thermal stratification and broader operational temperature 
ranges, enabling more consistent water temperatures. Studies have 
highlighted that the latent heat storage of PCMs significantly reduces 
temperature fluctuations and enhances the thermal response of SWH 
systems under intermittent solar conditions [ 79 , 80 ]. For instance, 
paraffin-based PCMs with melting points in the 50 – 70 
◦
C range, when 
encapsulated in aluminum or polymer shells, have shown effective 
thermal buffering in both laboratory and field tests [ 81 ]. Overall, the 
choice of PCM type, encapsulation strategy, and integration point within 
the system plays a critical role in determining the long-term reliability, 
energy savings, and cost-effectiveness of PCM-enhanced solar water 
heaters.

3.1.1. PCM nano-composites
PCMs are frequently changed into composite PCMs, so that charac -
teristics of heat transfer can be improved. A PCM composite is made up 
of a phase change substance, a shape-stabilizing substance (e.g., poly -
mer or ceramic) and a thermally conductive additive (such as graphite). 
The solidification time is reduced and thermal conductivity could be 
improved by using this method. Increase in thermal conductivity is 
correlated with increased nanoparticle ratios. Aside from the positives, 
they bring with them new hurdles such as increase in cost, compatibility 
problem, stability issues and increased difficulty [ 86 ]. In addition to this 
PCMs offer an intriguing option for addressing challenges related to TES. 
In particular, there is a growing interest in nanoparticle-enhanced PCMs 
[ 87 ].
3.1.2. PCM with carbon-based nanoparticles
In a study the fins and MWCNTs have been used to create a scalable

show that a PCM-based system using MWCNTs and fins may produce 
water temperatures of up to 79.0 
◦

3.1.3. PCM with silver nanoparticle
The use of silver nanoparticles into PCMs for solar energy applica -

nanoparticle concentration in phase transition materials improves TES.

consideration in order to enhance the performance of solar energy sys -

thermal characteristics of an organic ester PCM that was infused with

properties and heat storage capabilities. Consequently, this develop -
ment holds promise for utilization in solar TES applications [ 93 ]. 
Another recent work, focuses on the experimental investigation of

enhancing their energy storage capacity. The study included the prep -

enhancing energy storage capabilities, mostly attributed to improved 
intermolecular attraction. The composites containing scattered Ag 
nanoparticles exhibited thermal stability up to a temperature of 220 
◦
C, 
with a small improvement in thermal stability compared to the com -

the solar radiation absorptivity and thermal behavior of salt hydrate

nanoparticles will have improved optical and thermal behavior [ 16 ]. It

3.2.1. Microencapsulation of PCM
PCMs are increasingly gaining popularity and recognition in the field 
of TES. Microcapsules enhance the mechanical and thermal properties of 
PCMs used for TES by increasing the heat transfer area and minimising 
the seepage of melted materials. The use of PCM encapsulation is 
necessary in order to prevent leakage of PCM, enhance heat transfer, and 
effectively manage and minimise the reactivity of PCM with the sur -
rounding environment. In recent times, a considerable body of study has 
emerged about the use of PCM microcapsules in energy systems, with 
several studies being conducted to elucidate their practical applications 
[ 115 ]. The examination focuses on the utilization of microencapsulation 
techniques used to bioderived PCM beeswax, with an ethyl cellulose 
shell, for the purpose of TES applications. Beeswax is a naturally 
occurring PCM that has a significant phase transition enthalpy, which is 
equivalent to that of synthesized PCMs. The emulsion solvent evapora -
tion technique was used to shape-stabilize beeswax using the 
biopolymer ethyl cellulose. The microcapsules that have been synthe -
sized consist of sustainable materials and have a significant TES capa -
bility. The synthetic microcapsules have the potential to serve for TES 
applications [ 113 ]. To improve TES capacity, researchers have recently 
investigated microencapsulating bio-based PCM with an inorganic shell 
covered with silica. The cenospheres is used for the encapsulation of a 
cost-effective, bio-derived PCM obtained from processed edible vege -
table oil. The results of this study support the idea that cenospheres 
might serve as an inorganic covering for PCMs and that they could one 
day be used into construction materials for TES [ 116 ]. The development 
of scalable and cost-effective encapsulation systems that make use of 
eco-friendly shell materials requires additional research. It ’ s possible 
that hybrid shells, which combine organic and inorganic components, 
could provide a more equal approach. In addition, lifecycle analysis, 
mechanical performance, and integration with host materials (such as 
cement and polymers) are to be focused for future applications of TES.
3.2.2. Nanoencapsulation of PCM
The limited thermal stability, poor thermal conductivity, and liquid 
nature of PCMs provide obstacles in direct application. The PCM may be 
shielded from outside effects, given more surface area for heat trans -
mission, and made possible by encasing them in shell material, which 
also enables mobile thermal energy storage devices [ 117 ]. Nano -
encapsulation has emerged as a very promising approach for enhancing 
the efficacy of PCMs, including both organic and inorganic variants. The 
aforementioned phenomenon facilitates the augmentation of a sub -
stantial specific surface area, impedes the interchange of the enclosed 
substance with the surrounding environment, regulates thermal transfer 
via the capsule membrane, and triggers corresponding melting or crys -
tallisation owing to the diminutive size of the core [ 109 ]. An investi -
gation focuses of on the preparation of nano-encapsulated PCM 
emulsions using a solvent-assisted approach, with the aim of exploring 
their potential applications in solar energy systems for efficient energy 
storage with better PCM stability [ 109 ]. Researchers have done exami -
nation on the preparation and characterization of nano-encapsulated 
n-tetradecane, which serves as a PCM for TES. The use of nano -
capsules has the potential to be employed in the context of TES and the 
increase of heat transport [ 118 , 119 ]. It is evident that, choosing the 
right encapsulation size is essential for achieving efficient and 
long-lasting thermal energy storage in solar water heating systems. 
Fig. 9 illustrates the three main types of PCM encapsulation based on 
capsule size: nano, micro, and macro-encapsulation. Nano- -
encapsulation (less than 1 μ m) allows for quick heat transfer due to its 
high surface area, while micro-encapsulation (1 – 1000 μ m) offers a good 
balance between thermal performance and structural stability. 
Macro-encapsulation (greater than 1 mm) holds larger amounts of PCM 
but tends to have slower heat exchange rates. In each case, the protective 
shell helps maintain the integrity of the PCM and prevents leakage.
Electrohydrodynamic processing is one exclusive technique utilised 
for PCM material encapsulation. This type of processing has shown to be 
effective for encapsulating a variety of components, including PCMs, 
biomedical compounds, functional food additives, and other chemicals, 
in polymer matrices [ 117 ]. Another technique using nano-encapsulated 
PCM for improved performance is the sol-gel approach. In this regard a 
study focuses on the synthesis, characterization, and thermal charac -
teristics of nanoencapsulated phase transition materials using the sol-gel 
technique. The PCM nanocapsules consist of a core material composed 
of palmitic acid (PA) and a shell material composed of SiO

5. Enhancement methods of PCM-based TES
In recent years, researchers have been actively working to improve 
the performance of PCM-based thermal energy storage (TES) systems 
used in solar water heating. One of the main challenges with PCMs is 
their low thermal conductivity, which limits how quickly they can 
absorb and release heat. To address this, several enhancement methods 
have been developed. A common strategy is to mix the PCM with ma -
terials that conduct heat better such as metal fins, porous metal foams, 
or nanoparticles like copper oxide, aluminum oxide, or graphene. These 
additives help the PCM melt and solidify more quickly, making the 
storage system more efficient. For example, studies in top-tier journals 
like Renewable Energy and Applied Thermal Engineering have shown 
that adding graphene or copper nanoparticles to paraffin wax can speed 
up the heat transfer process by 30 % – 50 % [ 134 ]. Another approach 
focuses on improving the design of the storage container. By changing 
the shape or adding internal structures like fins or layers, the system can 
transfer heat more effectively. There ’ s also growing interest in using 
composite PCMs by combining different materials or creating eutectic 
mixtures, researchers can fine-tune the thermal properties for better 
performance. Encapsulation techniques, such as micro and 
macro-encapsulation, are also being used to prevent leakage and ensure 
long-term stability. These combined efforts are making PCM-based TES 
systems more practical, efficient, and suitable for everyday use in solar

5.2. Modified evacuated tube collector
Modified evacuated tube collectors (ETCs) have emerged as a highly 
efficient solution for integrating PCMs into SWH systems. Traditional 
ETCs are known for their superior heat retention due to vacuum insu -
lation, but recent modifications have aimed to further enhance their 
thermal performance and storage capabilities. These improvements 
typically involve structural changes such as incorporating finned inner 
tubes, U-tube configurations, or concentric tube designs to increase the 
surface area for heat transfer. More advanced designs embed PCM 
capsules or jackets within or around the inner tube, allowing for direct 
heat absorption and extended heat release even after sunset. Literatures 
show that such modifications can significantly boost the thermal effi -
ciency of ETCs, in some cases achieving up to 20 – 30 % more effective 
heat retention compared to unmodified designs [ 145 ]. A modified 
design of evacuated tube collector has been used for domestic solar 
water heater. Myristic acid is taken as heat storage material. The main 
benefit of this design is that the incorporation of myristic acid in the 
manifold does not impact the vacuum tube ’ s strength and does not take 
up extra roof space. Myristic acid ’ s excellent thermal and chemical 
characteristics increase system performance. The higher and lower flow 
rates during both charging procedures are advised for high daily effi -
ciency and high hot water temperature, respectively. The modified 
design meets the hot water demand in the late evening and reduces the 
risk of overheating as compared to the standard one [ 144 ]. Overall, 
modified ETCs provide a more reliable and energy-efficient option for 
solar water heating, especially in regions with fluctuating solar radiation 
or high evening water demand.
5.3. PCM with hybrid nano particle
One of the promising recent advancements in PCM-based thermal 
energy storage systems is the incorporation of hybrid nanoparticles to 
enhance thermal conductivity and overall performance. Unlike single 
nanoparticles, hybrid nanomaterials combine two or more types of 
nanoparticles such as metal oxides and carbon-based to achieve 
synergistic effects. These hybrid nanofillers improve not only the ther -
mal conductivity but also the heat distribution within the PCM, leading 
to faster charging and discharging cycles. Studies have shown that 
hybrid-enhanced PCMs can offer up to 60 – 80 % improvement in thermal 
conductivity compared to pure PCMs. In a recently conducted study, the 
concept of hybrid nanoparticle based PCM in FPSWH has been exam -
ined. The hybrid nano PCM (paraffin wax) has been developed using 
various weight percentages of MWCNTs and nano SiO
2
. Paraffin wax ’ s 
thermal conductivity was 0.24 W/m-K, but for hybrid nano PCM, it rose 
to 0.47719 W/m-K. Collector efficiency with hybrid nano PCM is found

and maintaining the latent heat capacity of the PCM. These findings 
suggest that hybrid nanomaterials offer a balanced and efficient way to 
overcome the limitations of conventional PCMs, making them a prom -

6. Melting and solidification characteristics of PCMs

7. Technical challenges of PCM/NePCM integrated solar water 
heater
Organic PCMs have a low thermal conductivity, which indicates that 
the device is not very efficient because of inefficient charging and 
discharging times [ 157 ]. In order to improve the thermophysical char -
acteristics of PCM, different nanoparticles are added [ 158 ]. It has been 
found that while this improves thermal conductivity, it also presents 
problems, including higher viscosity and the possibility of nanoparticle 
fusion. Additionally, it is discovered that when thermal cycling pro -
cedures are repeated, solar water heaters based on PCM/NePCM do not 
exhibit stability. Over time, repeated cycles of melting and solidification 
may cause PCM characteristics to deteriorate, which could impair the 
thermal storage system ’ s long-term performance. Degradation over time 
may result from certain PCMs ’ corrosive or chemically reactive prop -
erties with typical building materials [ 159 ]. Some PCMs, particularly 
inorganic types such as salt hydrates, are susceptible to phase separation 
and supercooling, which is the tendency to remain liquid below the 
freezing point when subjected to thermal cycling [ 160 ]. These occur -
rences may result in less effective energy storage and uneven thermal 
performance. Phase transitions also cause volume changes in PCMs, 
which can put stress on containment structures. Solar water heater tanks 
and storage tubes may lose their structural integrity due to frequent 
expansion and contraction. Certain PCMs are poisonous or flammable, 
which makes their use dangerous. Selecting safe and non-toxic PCMs, 
such as some paraffins, is essential for household hot water applications 
to avoid health risks in the event of leaks or system malfunctions. The 
design of solar water heaters becomes more complex when PCMs are 
used, necessitating the installation of other parts like control systems or 
heat exchangers. Technical Challenges of PCM/NePCM-Integrated Solar

this, improvements like adding fins, encasing PCMs, or utilizing 
high-conductivity materials like porous media have been investigated 
[ 161 ]. Utilizing nucleating chemicals or advanced encapsulation 
methods to address phase separation and supercooling problems. If there

effective use of a wide variety of PCMs in diverse SWH configurations, 
including flat plate solar water heaters (FPSWH), evacuated tube col -

rating nano-PCM in aluminum containers, or using PCMs in steel balls, 
have demonstrated prolonged thermal retention, keeping water tem -
peratures significantly higher than ambient conditions for extended 
durations. Furthermore, exergy efficiency improvements of over 5 % 
have been reported in systems using nano-enhanced paraffin wax. 
Overall, the integration of PCM in SWH systems leads to enhanced en -
ergy storage capacity, improved efficiency, stabilized output tempera -
tures, and reduced operational fluctuations, thereby reinforcing the 
value of PCM-based thermal energy storage for both domestic and

9. Performance metrics and practical considerations
Several recent studies have highlighted the importance of system 
geometry and PCM type in improving the thermal response of solar 
water heating systems. Among various PCMs, paraffin wax has consis -
tently shown better performance when paired with enhanced heat 
transfer structures. For example, introducing a combination of trian -
gular and rectangular fins within the thermal storage cavity has led to a 
notable increase in charging speed, while round and longitudinal fins 
have similarly reduced both melting and solidification times. More 
complex fin designs such as dendritic or tree-shaped structures have 
proven even more effective. These configurations have significantly 
shortened the discharging durations in systems using lauric acid and 
water as PCMs, with some studies reporting over 50 % improvement in 
heat release time. Other geometric enhancements, like helical fins or 
specialized heat exchangers such as U-shaped and multi-tube configu -
rations, have also shown substantial reductions in charging times. One 
of the most promising strategies involves combining metal foam with 
fins inside cylindrical shells, which has yielded dramatic reductions in 
both heat storage and release durations. Overall, the comparative find -
ings suggest that carefully tailoring the internal geometry of PCM-based 
systems is key to enhancing thermal performance and ensuring more 
efficient, responsive solar water heating applications. A comparative 
studies of improvement in charging and discharging duration on solar 
water heating systems is explained in Table 7 . These studies represent 
the improvement in charging and discharging duration on solar water 
heating system utilizing PCMs.The process of absorbing and releasing 
thermal energy during a phase transition is known as charging and 
discharging in a PCM. The PCM stores energy as latent heat when it 
melts and absorbs heat during charging. On the other hand, the PCM 
releases heat and solidifies during discharging, releasing the energy that 
has been stored. Increasing the heat transfer rate is essential for reducing 
the charging and discharging times of PCMs. In general, charging and 
discharging durations are influenced by PCM properties and system 
geometry.
The integration of phase change materials (PCMs) with innovative 
system enhancements has shown a clear positive impact on both the 
thermal efficiency and cost-effectiveness of solar water heating systems. 
Various studies highlight that combining PCMs with features such as 
rotating drums, external condensers, ultrasonic humidifiers, and 
photovoltaic-powered heaters can significantly improve daily efficiency. 
For instance, a solar still using saline water along with a rotating drum 
and copper oxide nanofluids achieved an impressive efficiency of 85.5 
%, while a system incorporating paraffin wax with photovoltaic and 
Peltier components reached 80 %. In comparison, conventional systems 
typically operate at much lower efficiencies, ranging from 34 % to 50 %. 
In addition to performance gains, several modified systems also 
demonstrated cost advantages. A system utilizing paraffin wax with 
photovoltaic support reduced the cost of freshwater production to 
$0.008/L, slightly lower than the $0.009/L of the traditional setup. 
Similarly, a tray-type solar still improved not only in efficiency but also 
reduced production cost to $0.021/L, compared to $0.028/L in a con -
ventional design. These findings collectively indicate that well-designed 
PCM-based solar water heating systems can deliver both improved 
thermal performance and economic viability, making them attractive 
solutions for sustainable energy applications. The summary of recent 
works on daily efficiency and cost analysis of solar water heating sys -
tems have been compiled in Table 8 . The table represent overview of the 
daily efficiency and cost analysis of solar water heating systems. A solar 
heating system ’ s daily efficiency is a measure of how well sunshine is 
converted into useful heat. The design of the system, the amount of 
sunlight present, and the surrounding temperature are some of the 
variables that affect this efficiency.Furthermore, the cost of the tested 
stills depends on the kind of still and its parts. This will help determine 
the energy savings and payback period of investing in a less energy- 
intensive system, which is probably going to cost more to purchase.
10. Conclusions
Phase Change Materials are emerging as a key enabler for improving 
the efficiency and sustainability of solar water heating systems. Their 
unique ability to store thermal energy as latent heat during phase 
transitions helps balance fluctuations in solar energy availability, 
enabling more consistent and reliable hot water supply. Among various 
PCM types, organic materials like paraffin wax are widely used for low- 
temperature applications due to their chemical stability and ease of 
integration. However, real-world implementation still faces challenges 
such as low thermal conductivity, flammability, material degradation, 
and high costs. Recent research and experimental insights offer 
encouraging solutions to these limitations. Incorporating nanoparticles, 
using extended surfaces such as fins, embedding PCMs within metal 
foams, and applying advanced encapsulation techniques have all shown 
to significantly boost heat transfer rates and shorten charging/dis -
charging cycles. These enhancements not only improve system respon -
siveness but also increase overall energy conversion efficiency 
translating into reduced reliance on auxiliary energy sources and sub -
stantial carbon emission reductions.
Furthermore, thoughtful system design such as modified collector 
geometries, hybrid heat exchangers, and the strategic placement of 
PCMs has proven to enhance energy retention and delivery. The

11. Future scopes
The potential for further advances and broad implementation of 
solar water heaters utilizing PCMs is substantial. In order to advance this 
technology, it is necessary to thoroughly investigate and conduct 
research in numerous crucial areas: 
• Enhancing PCM Performance: It is important to concentrate on 
enhancing the thermal conductivity, particularly in the case of 
organic versions. Advancements in nano composites, sophisticated 
encapsulation processes, and the integration of innovative materials 
have the potential to address current constraints, resulting in 
enhanced energy storage efficiency.
• Development of Cost-Effective PCM Solutions: The exorbitant expenses 
linked to specific PCMs continue to be a significant obstacle. Future 
research should focus on the advancement of economical alterna -
tives that maintain high performance standards. One possible 
approach is to investigate novel materials, synthesis techniques, or 
large-scale production methods to enhance the economic feasibility 
of PCM-based solar water heaters.
• Safety and Environmental Considerations: It is imperative to prioritise 
addressing the safety concerns related to organic PCMs, particularly 
in terms of fire safety, in order to achieve wider acceptability. Future 
research should focus on the development of ecologically sound and 
secure PCMs to guarantee the long-term viability of solar water 
heaters.
• Integration of Advanced Materials: Advanced materials, such as carbon 
nanotubes and nanoadditions, can significantly improve the perfor -
mance of inorganic salt hydrates. Research in this field should 
investigate innovative methods to address difficulties like as super -
cooling, phase segregation, and corrosiveness.
To summarise, the future potential of solar water heaters with PCMs 
requires a comprehensive approach that includes several fields such as 
material science, engineering, and environmental issues. Uninterrupted 
advancement and cooperative research endeavours can facilitate the 
development of PCM-based solar water heating systems that are more 
environmentally friendly, economically efficient, and generally 
embraced.
