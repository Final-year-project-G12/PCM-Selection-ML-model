ABSTRACT Renewable energy systems, such as solar, wind, and hybrid sources, present complex
forecasting challenges due to their stochastic and weather-dependent nature. The growing availability of
heterogeneous and complementary data modalities including meteorological forecasts, satellite imagery,
numerical sensor streams, and grid interaction logs has motivated the application of multimodal learning
techniques to improve time series forecasting accuracy. Unlike previous surveys that either focused narrowly
on single-modality forecasting or provided only high-level overviews, this paper offers a focused and
technically grounded review of multimodal learning strategies specific to renewable energy forecasting. We
categorize and compare key fusion strategies (early, late, hybrid), analyze deep model architectures (e.g., co-
attention and transformer-based fusion), and discuss deployment-related challenges such as data alignment,
modality missingness, and interpretability. Then, this paper reviews multimodal learning techniques based
on four main aspects, i.e., classify fusion techniques, examine model architectures, showcase practical
application scenarios, and summarize benchmark datasets. Finally, the paper identifies existing research gaps
and outlines promising future directions in this rapidly evolving field, such as self-supervised multimodal
learning and adaptive fusion under uncertainty.

energy sources has accelerated the deployment of solar,
wind, and hybrid energy systems [1]. Accurate forecasting
of energy generation from these sources is essential to
maintain grid stability, optimize operational efficiency, and
enable effective participation in the energy market [2].
Unlike conventional energy generation, renewable energy
sources are inherently intermittent and non-linear, driven
by a complex combination of meteorological variables,
geographic conditions, and temporal patterns of energy
demand [3]. As a result, accurate forecasting remains a

II. BACKGROUND
This section briefly introduces the renewable energy (RE)
context in which multimodal learning is applied, with empha-
sis on the diverse data modalities (e.g., weather observations,
satellite imagery, and numerical predictions) relevant to
forecasting tasks. It outlines the core forecasting challenges
posed by renewable systems, such as nonstationarity, missing

A. RENEWABLE ENERGY SYSTEMS AND ASSOCIATED
DATA MODALITIES
Modern renewable energy systems, particularly solar PV ,
wind farms, and their hybrid integrations, generate substantial
volumes of heterogeneous data. These include ground-based
sensor measurements (e.g., global horizontal irradiance,
wind speed), numerical weather prediction (NWP) outputs,
satellite imagery, spatial-geographical features, and historical
power generation records. Each of these modalities offers
complementary perspectives for forecasting energy output.
However, the inherent intermittency and variability of these
systems present forecasting challenges that cannot be suffi-
ciently addressed using any single data modality alone [16],
[17], [18].
B. FORECASTING CHALLENGES AND THE NEED FOR
MUL TIMODAL MODELS
Time series forecasting in renewable energy systems is
inherently complex due to the stochastic nature of weather-
driven generation. Key challenges include: (1) high temporal
and spatial variability, (2) nonlinear relationships between
inputs and outputs, (3) missing or noisy data due to
sensor failures, and (4) site-specific dynamics that reduce
generalizability [19], [20], [21]. Traditional single-modality
models, e.g., those relying solely on historical generation
or on-site weather data, often fail to capture these complex

C. MUL TIMODAL LEARNING: DEFINITION AND
APPLICATION IN ENERGY FORECASTING
Multimodal learning refers to machine learning techniques
that integrate information from multiple data modalities
such as time-series meteorological data, satellite imagery,
and NWP outputs to produce richer and more robust
representations [25]. In renewable energy forecasting, mul-
timodal learning enables models to go beyond local sen-
sor data and incorporate broader spatial and predictive
context. For instance, cloud motion captured via satellite
imagery can enhance solar forecasting when combined
with irradiance measurements, while NWP-based wind
maps improve predictions when fused with turbine-level
telemetry [13], [26].
Fusion strategies in multimodal learning vary in com-
plexity: early fusion concatenates raw features; intermediate
fusion employs shared network layers or attention mecha-
nisms; and late fusion aggregates model outputs. Each strat-
egy has trade-offs in terms of interpretability, performance,
and computational cost [27]. Despite challenges such as data
alignment and heterogeneous resolution, multimodal models
have shown promise in handling extreme conditions, adapting
to new locations, and maintaining performance during partial
data loss [24].
In this paper, we survey recent advances in multimodal
learning for renewable energy forecasting, with an emphasis
on deep fusion architectures and applications across solar,
wind, and hybrid systems.

B. WEATHER FORECASTS
Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) models provide struc-

solar and wind power prediction. They solve physical equa-

of variables like temperature, humidity, wind speed, cloud
cover, and solar radiation [44].
Common NWP outputs used in renewables include:
• 2 m air and surface temperature.
• Relative humidity affecting irradiance transmission.
• Wind speed/direction at turbine hub heights.
• Cloud cover by altitude layers.
• Precipitation and pressure relevant for turbine safety.
• Solar radiation fluxes such as GHI and DNI [45].
NWP forecasts are often interpolated to plant locations via
geospatial methods (IDW, kriging).

• Ambiguity and Variability in Terminology: Different

IV. MULTIMODAL LEARNING STRATEGIES
A. EARL Y FUSION: FEATURE CONCATENATION AT INPUT
LEVEL
Early fusion is one of the simplest yet widely adopted
strategies in multimodal learning. It involves combining
features from multiple data modalities at the input level to
form a single unified representation [27], [75], which is then
passed to a downstream model. This approach assumes that
all relevant information from different modalities can be
effectively captured and leveraged together without explicitly
modeling modality-specific interactions.
In renewable energy forecasting, early fusion typically
entails concatenating meteorological variables (e.g., wind
speed, temperature, humidity), temporal descriptors (e.g.,
time of day, seasonal indices), and spatial features (e.g.,
elevation, latitude/longitude) into a single input vector. This
fused vector is subsequently used to train models such
as multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), random forests, or deep
neural networks [76].
Despite its conceptual simplicity, early fusion faces key
challenges in handling heterogeneous and unaligned data. For
instance, numerical weather prediction (NWP) features are
often continuous and temporally dense, whereas geographic
metadata can be categorical or sparse, requiring careful
preprocessing and embedding techniques to harmonize
inputs [77]. Furthermore, concatenation may dilute crucial
cross-modal interactions, especially in high-dimensional
spaces, risking the dominance of noisy or irrelevant features.
Nevertheless, early fusion remains popular due to its com-
putational efficiency and straightforward implementation.
Recent deep learning models improve on naive concatenation
by first passing each modality through dedicated encoders
or embedding layers before merging, thus enhancing repre-
sentational richness and facilitating learning from correlated
multimodal patterns [78].
Techniques such as feature selection, dimensionality
reduction (e.g., PCA, autoencoders), and regularization
(dropout, batch normalization) are commonly applied to
improve early fusion robustness and generalization. Figure 3
presents a redesigned flowchart of the early fusion strategy,
emphasizing the separate preprocessing of modalities before
concatenation, which supports clearer understanding and
replicability.
B. LATE FUSION: COMBINING PREDICTIONS FROM
MODALITY-SPECIFIC MODELS
Late fusion, or decision-level fusion, combines outputs from
independently trained modality-specific models rather than
merging raw features [75], [79]. Each modality is processed
separately, and their predictions are aggregated through
heuristics (e.g., averaging, weighted voting) or meta-learning
methods like stacking [80].

In renewable energy forecasting, late fusion pipelines com-

Late fusion provides significant advantages: modularity
allows independent training and updating of models; special-

RNNs for sequences); and robustness arises from graceful
degradation if a modality is missing or corrupted. However,
late fusion requires synchronization mechanisms, such as
interpolation or buffering, to reconcile different temporal
resolutions across modalities [81].
While late fusion methods do not inherently learn
cross-modal feature dependencies, incorporating a meta-
learner to weigh predictions adaptively can partially address
this limitation by exploiting context or reliability cues [80].
Careful validation and regularization are necessary to prevent
overfitting in stacked or ensemble models trained on limited
data.

C. HYBRID/INTERMEDIATE FUSION: ATTENTION-BASED
AND CO-LEARNING ARCHITECTURES
Hybrid or intermediate fusion integrates modalities at
latent representation levels within neural networks [75],
[82]. Attention mechanisms, gating units, and shared
latent spaces enable dynamic, context-sensitive interactions
between modalities.
Attention-based models allow selective focus on relevant
temporal or spatial features across modalities. For example,
in solar forecasting, attention can emphasize satellite image
regions with cloud activity while highlighting meteorological
time points with rapid humidity changes.
This strategy captures conditional and complex inter-
modal dependencies, adapting fusion weights dynamically
based on context critical for renewable energy where
relationships vary with geography and time [8]. Co-
learning approaches further enhance fusion by aligning
modality-specific features via bilinear pooling or tensor
fusion, promoting joint latent space discovery.
Though hybrid fusion increases model complexity and
training requirements, it offers improved interpretability
through attention visualization and gating activations. Fig-
ure 5 presents the enhanced hybrid fusion flowchart, clari-
fying intermediate feature extraction, attention modules, and
joint decoding.
D. CROSS-MODAL ATTENTION AND CO-ATTENTION
MECHANISMS
Cross-modal attention explicitly models interdependencies
between modalities [83]. Unlike unimodal attention, it allows
queries from one modality to attend to keys and values in
another, enabling fine-grained contextual alignment.
In renewable energy, this could involve satellite cloud
data modulating the relevance of temperature readings or
topographical maps informing wind sensor data interpreta-
tion. Co-attention further symmetrizes this interaction, with
modalities mutually attending to one another.
Transformer-based and multimodal BERT models have
adopted these mechanisms successfully in vision-language
tasks and are increasingly applied to renewable energy fore-
casting, showing gains in accuracy, missing data robustness,
and interpretability [84].
The computational overhead of attention layers is signif-
icant, particularly for high-dimensional modalities. Sparse
and hierarchical attention mechanisms help manage this
complexity.

V. MODEL ARCHITECTURES
A. TRADITIONAL MACHINE LEARNING WITH
ENGINEERED MUL TIMODAL FEATURES
Traditional machine learning approaches such as linear
regression, support vector machines (SVMs), and random
forests have historically contributed to renewable energy
forecasting by utilizing handcrafted features derived from
different modalities (e.g., temperature, solar irradiance, wind
speed, humidity). These features are typically concatenated
into a single multimodal feature vector:
x = [x(1), x(2), . . . ,x(M )]⊤,
where each x(i) is extracted from a specific modality. The
model estimates a target variable y (e.g., power output) as:
y = f (x) + ε.
These methods depend heavily on domain-specific feature
engineering to capture relevant patterns, such as lagged
variables and seasonal trends. While computationally effi-
cient, they often fail to capture nonlinear interactions and
dynamic cross-modal dependencies inherent in renewable
energy systems [87]. Therefore, the focus of recent research
has shifted toward deep multimodal models capable of
automatic representation learning.

VI. APPLICATIONS IN RENEWABLE ENERGY

A. SOLAR PV POWER FORECASTING USING IRRADIANCE,
CLOUD IMAGERY, AND WEATHER DATA
Solar photovoltaic (PV) power forecasting plays a crucial role
in optimizing grid operations and energy management due to
the intermittent and weather-dependent nature of solar power
generation. Accurate prediction relies on integrating multiple
heterogeneous data sources such as irradiance measurements,
satellite or ground-based cloud imagery, and meteorological
variables including temperature, humidity, and wind speed.
Irradiance G directly correlates with the PV output power
P. A simplified physical model for power generation can be
expressed as:
P = η · A · G,
where η denotes the panel efficiency and A the panel area.
However, the effective irradianceG at the PV module surface
is influenced by atmospheric conditions, cloud cover, aerosol
concentration, and shading effects, leading to highly variable
and nonlinear behavior.
Cloud imagery provides rich spatial-temporal information
on cloud movements and density. Deep learning methods,
particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have
been employed to extract features from satellite images or
ground-based sky cameras. These features capture cloud
dynamics that directly affect irradiance fluctuations. Given a
sequence of cloud images at times t, t − 1, . . . ,t − n, models
can forecast future irradiance Gt+τ or directly predict PV
power output at horizon τ .
Multimodal fusion approaches effectively integrate numer-
ical weather predictions (NWP), irradiance sensors, and
image-derived cloud features to enhance forecasting accuracy
over various horizons from intra-hour nowcasting to day-
ahead predictions [58]. These fusion frameworks often use
hybrid architectures combining CNNs for image processing

interpretability and computational efficiency for operational
deployment [101].
Overall, leveraging irradiance measurements, cloud

B. DATASET QUALITY AND MODALITY COMPLETENESS
The performance and generalizability of forecasting
models strongly depend on the quality of training and
evaluation datasets. Several factors must be considered
to ensure reliable model development and deployment,
including:
• Data Accuracy and Precision: Sensor calibration
and measurement errors can introduce noise, biasing
forecasts. Ensuring high-quality instrumentation and
data cleaning is vital.
• Sampling Rate and Temporal Resolution: High-
frequency data captures rapid fluctuations but increases
computational burden. Forecasting horizons dictate
suitable temporal resolutions.
• Completeness and Missing Data: Multimodal datasets
often suffer from missing values due to sensor faults or
transmission errors. Strategies like imputation or robust
modeling are needed.
• Synchronization Across Modalities: Temporal align-
ment of heterogeneous data sources (e.g., weather
maps, sensor readings, images) is necessary to preserve
correlations and improve fusion.
• Dataset Size and Diversity: Sufficient volume and
variety of scenarios (seasonal changes, geographic
diversity) improve model robustness.
To ensure reproducibility and scalability, benchmarks
should report metadata completeness, alignment statistics,
and noise levels across modalities. Tools such as modality
correlation matrices and temporal heatmaps are increasingly
used for dataset validation.

VIII. CHALLENGES IN MULTIMODAL FORECASTING FOR
RENEWABLE ENERGY
A. SPATIOTEMPORAL RESOLUTION MISMATCH ACROSS
MODALITIES
One of the principal challenges in multimodal renewable
energy forecasting arises from the mismatch in spatial
and temporal resolution among the diverse data modal-
ities. Renewable energy systems integrate heterogeneous
data sources, including satellite imagery, weather station
measurements, sensor arrays on turbines and solar panels,
and numerical weather prediction outputs. Each of these data
streams may differ substantially in spatial granularity and
temporal frequency [126].
For example, satellite imagery might be available only
every 15 minutes to an hour and cover broad geographic
regions, whereas turbine sensor data can be collected at
sub-second intervals but localized to individual units. Simi-
larly, weather station data might provide point measurements
at hourly or sub-hourly intervals, while NWP models output
gridded forecasts at resolutions ranging from a few kilometers
to tens of kilometers, updated every few hours.
This disparity leads to difficulties in fusing data effectively.
Direct concatenation or naive interpolation can introduce
artifacts or lose critical information. Temporal mismatch
may cause delays or phase shifts in the signal alignment,
reducing forecasting accuracy. Spatial resolution differences
complicate modeling, as fine-grained local effects might be
smoothed out in coarse-grained data, and vice versa.
To address these issues, various strategies have been
proposed. Spatial and temporal interpolation methods, such
as kriging or spline-based approaches, can resample data
to common grids or timestamps. More advanced methods
exploit spatiotemporal neural networks that learn to repre-
sent different resolutions jointly. Furthermore, hierarchical
or multi-scale models process modalities at their native
resolutions and integrate information through learned atten-
tion mechanisms. Nonetheless, spatiotemporal resolution
mismatch remains a significant bottleneck in building

robust multimodal forecasting models, requiring careful
pre-processing, domain knowledge, and adaptive modeling
techniques.
B. DATA ALIGNMENT, MISSING MODALITIES, AND
ASYNCHRONOUS UPDATES
Multimodal forecasting systems often face challenges related
to data alignment and completeness. Since different sensors
and data providers operate independently, datasets may not
be synchronized perfectly in time or may contain gaps due
to sensor failures, communication errors, or maintenance
downtime.
Asynchronous updates mean that some modalities update
more frequently or irregularly compared to others. For
instance, weather satellite passes may be periodic but
sparse, whereas ground-based sensors can have continu-
ous streams. Combining these requires temporal alignment
strategies such as resampling, buffering, or window-based
aggregation.
Missing modalities pose another challenge: in practical
deployments, one or more data streams may become tem-
porarily unavailable. Models trained on complete multimodal
inputs may perform poorly or fail when some inputs are
missing, limiting real-world applicability.
Handling these issues requires robust data imputation
and missing data modeling techniques. Statistical imputation
methods, such as mean or interpolation-based filling, are
simple but may not capture complex dependencies. More
advanced approaches employ machine learning models
trained to reconstruct missing modalities from available data,
including generative models like variational autoencoders or
adversarial networks.
Moreover, asynchronous data streams call for flexible
model architectures capable of processing variable-length
sequences and conditional inputs. Recurrent networks with
masking, attention mechanisms that weigh inputs dynam-
ically, and multi-task learning frameworks can improve
robustness [127]. Effective data alignment and missing
modality handling are critical to maintain forecasting accu-
racy and reliability, especially in operational energy systems
where sensor faults and network issues are common.
C. COMPUTATIONAL COMPLEXITY OF DEEP
MUL TIMODAL MODELS
The integration of multiple high-dimensional modalities in
renewable energy forecasting often leads to complex deep
learning models with large numbers of parameters and heavy
computational demands. Models such as convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) for image processing, recurrent neural
networks (RNNs) or transformers for temporal data, and
graph neural networks (GNNs) for spatial relationships must
be combined effectively, increasing architectural complex-
ity [128], [129].
Training these models requires extensive computational
resources, including high-memory GPUs or clusters, pro-
longed training times, and careful hyperparameter tuning.
This complexity can be prohibitive for researchers and
practitioners with limited access to such infrastructure.
At inference time, computational cost also affects real-time
deployment feasibility. Forecasting systems used by grid
operators or energy traders must produce timely predictions;
excessively heavy models may introduce latency or require
expensive hardware upgrades [126].
Strategies to reduce computational complexity include
model compression techniques such as pruning, quantiza-
tion, and knowledge distillation, which simplify trained
models without significant accuracy loss. Architectural
innovations like lightweight convolutional blocks, effi-
cient attention mechanisms, and parameter sharing also
help [129]. Additionally, modular architectures where
modality-specific encoders run in parallel, with a lightweight
fusion module, balance complexity and performance. Edge
computing and distributed processing approaches can
further alleviate centralized computational burdens [130].
Despite these advances, computational complexity remains
a core challenge in scaling multimodal forecasting sys-
tems to large geographic regions and fine temporal
resolutions.
D. LACK OF STANDARDIZED MUL TIMODAL BENCHMARKS
Another major obstacle in advancing multimodal renewable
energy forecasting is the absence of widely accepted
standardized benchmark datasets and evaluation protocols
that encompass multiple data modalities. While numerous
single-modality datasets exist for solar or wind forecasting
individually, fewer datasets integrate multiple modalities with
consistent annotation and accessibility [131].
This lack of benchmarks hinders the fair comparison of
competing algorithms and slows progress. Researchers often
rely on proprietary or limited datasets, leading to fragmented
efforts and difficulties reproducing results.
Creating comprehensive multimodal benchmark datasets
requires coordinated efforts to collect, preprocess, and
release synchronized multimodal data including satellite
imagery, ground sensors, meteorological variables, and
historical power outputs. These datasets should cover
diverse geographic regions, temporal scales, and weather
regimes to enable robust generalization. Furthermore,
standardized evaluation protocols specifying forecasting
horizons, train-test splits, and metrics are needed to ensure
comparability. Public competitions and challenges focused
on multimodal forecasting could catalyze community
engagement [126].
Efforts in other domains, such as multimodal video pro-
cessing and medical data analysis, provide useful paradigms.
However, the domain-specific constraints and complexities of
renewable energy systems require a customized benchmark
design. Addressing the lack of standardized multimodal
benchmarks is crucial to foster transparent, reproducible
research, and accelerate innovation in this field.

E. INTERPRETABILITY AND TRUST IN AI-BASED ENERGY
FORECASTING
The deployment of AI models for renewable energy fore-
casting in operational settings requires not only accuracy
but also interpretability and trustworthiness. Complex deep
multimodal models are often regarded as ‘‘black boxes,’’
limiting user understanding of their decision processes
and hampering acceptance by grid operators and energy
stakeholders [132].
Interpretability is essential for model debugging, regu-
latory compliance, and ensuring model robustness under
unexpected operating conditions. Energy system operators
need to trust that forecasts are reliable and understand the
influence of various inputs.
Several approaches have been developed to improve inter-
pretability of multimodal models. Attention mechanisms can
highlight which modalities or features are most influential
for a given prediction. Post-hoc explanation methods, such
as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME (Local
Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), provide local
feature importance.
Model simplification through surrogate models can aid
human comprehension, though sometimes at the expense of
accuracy. Incorporating domain knowledge and physically
interpretable modules enhances trustworthiness in energy
forecasting environments [133].
Furthermore, uncertainty quantification, such as prediction
intervals or Bayesian approaches, communicates confidence
levels, helping users make informed decisions [134].
Building interpretability and trust into AI-based renewable
energy forecasting models remains an ongoing challenge
but is critical for practical deployment and stakeholder
acceptance.

IX. FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
A. FOUNDATION MODELS TRAINED ON LARGE-SCALE
MUL TIMODAL ENERGY DATA
The rapid advancement of foundation models large-scale
pretrained neural networks capable of handling diverse tasks
with minimal fine-tuning presents a promising frontier for
renewable energy forecasting. These models, exemplified by
architectures like GPT, BERT, or vision-language transform-
ers, demonstrate remarkable ability to learn rich, transferable
representations from massive datasets spanning multiple
modalities [137].
Applying foundation models to renewable energy systems
entails pretraining on vast and heterogeneous multimodal
datasets that include satellite imagery, weather observations,
sensor telemetry, historical power outputs, and grid opera-
tional data. Such models can capture complex cross-modal
interactions, spatiotemporal dependencies, and underlying
physical phenomena in a unified latent space [8].
The benefits include improved generalization across
geographic regions and weather patterns, reduction in data
annotation effort through self-supervised learning objectives,
and enhanced adaptability to downstream tasks such as
anomaly detection, fault diagnosis, and scenario simulation.
Challenges remain in curating sufficiently large and
diverse datasets, designing architectures that effectively fuse
multimodal inputs, and managing the computational cost of
training and deploying foundation models. Moreover, domain
adaptation techniques must be developed to tailor foundation
models pretrained on general data to specific renewable
energy applications and regions.
Research on foundation models in this domain could
dramatically transform forecasting accuracy and reliability,
enabling more resilient and efficient energy systems.
B. FEDERATED MUL TIMODAL LEARNING ACROSS
RENEWABLE PLANT OPERATORS
Renewable energy data is often siloed within individual plant
operators due to privacy, commercial sensitivity, or regu-
latory restrictions, limiting the availability of large-scale
datasets for centralized training. Federated learning offers
a paradigm to collaboratively train multimodal forecasting
models across multiple distributed data owners without
sharing raw data [138].
In federated multimodal learning, each operator trains a
local model on its own multimodal dataset (e.g., sensor
readings, local weather data, satellite imagery). Periodically,
model updates or gradients are aggregated centrally or
in a decentralized manner to update a global model.
This approach preserves data privacy while leveraging the
collective knowledge from diverse geographic regions and
system configurations.
Technical challenges include handling heterogeneous
data distributions across operators, synchronizing updates
when modalities differ or are incomplete, and designing
communication-efficient protocols to reduce overhead [139].

C. FEW-SHOT AND TRANSFER LEARNING IN
MODALITY-SCARCE ENVIRONMENTS
While many renewable energy plants produce rich multi-
modal data, others may have limited sensing infrastructure or
face operational constraints that restrict the availability of cer-
tain modalities. Few-shot and transfer learning methodolo-
gies aim to overcome this scarcity by leveraging knowledge
learned from data-rich domains to improve performance in
data-poor settings. Few-shot learning techniques train models
to generalize from a very small number of labeled examples,
often through meta-learning or metric learning approaches.
For instance, a model pretrained on multimodal data from
large solar farms could be adapted rapidly to a smaller or
newly commissioned site with minimal additional data.
Transfer learning enables the reuse of pretrained feature
extractors or entire models on related tasks or domains,
reducing training time and data requirements. In the context
of modality scarcity, transfer learning can help models
compensate for missing inputs by relying on correlated or
substitute modalities [140].
Developing robust few-shot and transfer learning tech-
niques tailored to multimodal renewable energy forecasting
requires addressing domain shift issues, modality mismatch,
and temporal nonstationarities. Furthermore, interpretability
and uncertainty estimation are crucial to ensure reliable
adaptation.
Advances in this area would broaden the applicability
of sophisticated forecasting models to diverse operational
scenarios, accelerating the deployment of AI in renewable
energy systems globally.
D. EXPLAINABLE AND UNCERTAINTY-AWARE
MUL TIMODAL MODELS
For AI-driven renewable energy forecasting systems to gain
widespread acceptance, it is essential to develop models
that not only provide accurate predictions but also offer
explanations and quantify uncertainty in their outputs.
Explainable AI (XAI) methods elucidate how multimodal
inputs contribute to forecasts, enhancing user trust and facili-
tating model debugging. Techniques such as attention visual-
ization, feature importance scoring, and concept-based expla-
nations enable operators to understand the decision-making
process of complex deep learning models [141].
Simultaneously, uncertainty-aware modeling quantifies
confidence levels associated with predictions, distinguishing
between aleatoric uncertainty (inherent data noise) and
epistemic uncertainty (model knowledge gaps). Bayesian
neural networks, ensembles, and probabilistic deep learning
approaches provide principled frameworks for uncertainty
estimation.
Incorporating explainability and uncertainty quantification
is especially critical in safety-critical or economically sensi-
tive renewable energy operations where decisions based on
forecasts impact grid stability and financial planning.
Future research should focus on integrating these aspects
seamlessly into multimodal architectures, balancing inter-
pretability with predictive performance, and developing
visualization tools tailored to energy system operators.
E. INTEGRATION OF PHYSICS-INFORMED LEARNING
WITH DATA-DRIVEN FUSION
Renewable energy forecasting benefits from combining
physics-based models grounded in domain knowledge with
data-driven AI techniques that learn patterns from data.
Physics-informed machine learning (PIML) incorporates
physical constraints, conservation laws, or differential equa-
tions into the training of neural networks, improving model
generalization and interpretability.
Integrating PIML with multimodal fusion involves design-
ing architectures that incorporate prior knowledge about
atmospheric dynamics, solar radiation physics, or turbine
aerodynamics alongside multimodal observational data.
For instance, differential equation solvers or simplified
energy balance models can be embedded as layers within
deep networks or used as regularization terms in loss
functions. This fusion helps mitigate overfitting, enforce
physically plausible outputs, and enable extrapolation to
unseen conditions [142].
Key challenges include representing complex physical
phenomena at scales compatible with neural network train-

X. CONCLUSION
Accurate forecasting of renewable energy generation remains
a cornerstone for the efficient management and operation
of modern power systems. This survey has comprehensively
examined the emerging paradigm of multimodal forecasting
frameworks tailored for renewable energy systems such as
solar photovoltaic, wind farms, and hybrid installations.
By leveraging heterogeneous data sources, including mete-
orological measurements, satellite imagery, sensor telemetry,
and grid operational data, multimodal approaches provide a
more holistic understanding of the underlying dynamics that
govern renewable power output.
Traditional single-modality forecasting models often
struggle to capture the complex spatiotemporal patterns and
nonlinear relationships present in renewable energy systems.
Multimodal learning frameworks address these challenges
by integrating complementary information across modalities,
resulting in enhanced predictive accuracy and robustness.
For instance, the fusion of irradiance measurements with
cloud imagery and weather data significantly improves
solar PV power forecasting, while the incorporation of
turbine sensor data and numerical weather predictions
advances wind power estimates. The flexibility of mul-
timodal architectures, including early fusion, late fusion,
and hybrid strategies, enables tailored solutions that accom-
modate diverse data availability scenarios and application
needs.
The key advantages of integrating multiple modalities
extend beyond accuracy improvements. Multimodal frame-
works inherently enhance resilience to missing or noisy
data, as models can rely on redundant information channels.
They also enable the exploitation of cross-modal correlations
and the discovery of latent factors driving renewable energy
variability. Recent developments in deep learning architec-
tures such as convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural
networks (RNNs), transformers, variational autoencoders,
and graph neural networks have demonstrated impressive
capabilities in capturing intricate multimodal dependencies.
Moreover, advances in foundation models, federated learn-
ing, and physics-informed AI point to a promising future
where scalable, collaborative, and interpretable forecasting
systems will become the norm.
Despite these promising developments, several critical
challenges remain. Issues such as spatiotemporal resolution
mismatch, asynchronous and incomplete data streams, high
computational costs, and the lack of standardized multi-
modal benchmarks require ongoing research and innovation.
Furthermore, to fully realize the potential of multimodal
forecasting in real-world renewable energy systems, it is
imperative to develop robust models that balance predictive
performance with interpretability and uncertainty quantifi-
cation. Explainable AI techniques and uncertainty-aware
frameworks will be essential to build operator trust and enable
informed decision-making in grid management and energy
markets.
To guide future research, we explicitly highlight the
following key open challenges and promising directions:
• Standardized Benchmark Datasets and Evaluation
Protocols: There is a critical need to develop and adopt
comprehensive multimodal datasets with consistent
annotations and standardized evaluation metrics to
enable fair comparison, reproducibility, and accelerated
progress in the field.
• Spatiotemporal Resolution Mismatch and Asyn-
chronous Fusion: Future work must focus on designing
advanced data fusion methods that can effectively rec-
oncile heterogeneous spatial and temporal granularities,
as well as handle asynchronous and incomplete data
streams, to improve robustness and accuracy.
• Interpretability and Uncertainty Quantification:
Developing explainable multimodal forecasting models
that provide transparent reasoning and well-calibrated
uncertainty estimates is essential for practical deploy-
ment and gaining trust among grid operators and energy
stakeholders.
• Federated and Privacy-Preserving Learning: As data
privacy concerns grow, expanding federated learning
frameworks to multimodal renewable energy datasets
will enable collaborative model training across organi-
zations without sharing raw sensitive data.
• Integration of Physics-Informed and Data-Driven
Approaches: Hybrid modeling paradigms that embed
physical knowledge of renewable systems within
data-driven multimodal architectures offer a promising
route to improve generalization, interpretability, and
scientific consistency.
These directions collectively form a roadmap to tackle current
limitations and harness the full potential of multimodal
learning for sustainable, reliable, and efficient renewable
energy forecasting.
In summary, the fusion of diverse data modalities through
advanced machine learning techniques represents a transfor-
mative approach to renewable energy forecasting. Continued
research addressing the outlined gaps will be crucial to
support the accelerating integration of renewables, optimize
grid operations, and foster the transition to a resilient and
low-carbon energy future.
