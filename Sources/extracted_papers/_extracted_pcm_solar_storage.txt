ABSTRACT
The escalating global energy demand, coupled with the urgent need to combat climate change, underscores the 
necessity for effective and sustainable energy storage solutions. Phase change materials (PCMs) have emerged as 
a viable technology for thermal energy storage, particularly in solar energy applications, due to their ability to 
efficiently store and release thermal energy during phase transitions while maintaining a near-constant tem -
perature. This paper addresses the limitations of traditional thermal energy storage systems and explores the 
advancements in PCM integration within various solar energy systems. We discuss innovative methods to 
enhance heat transfer rates and thermal conductivity, including modifications of extended surfaces, heat pipes, 
cascading PCMs, encapsulation techniques, and the incorporation of nanoparticles. These enhancements can 
improve system performance by up to 73 %, with nanoparticle dispersion identified as the most economically 
viable solution. Additionally, we provide a comprehensive overview of the implementation of the artificial in -
telligence approach in optimizing PCM-based thermal energy storage systems, emphasizing the effectiveness of 
ensemble learning frameworks for accurate modeling. The review also highlights the development of nano-PCMs, 
which demonstrate significant improvements — 25.6 % in charging and 23.9 % in discharging rates — compared to 
conventional PCMs. Furthermore, we analyze the economic and environmental implications of PCM-based sys -
tems, focusing on critical issues such as carbon emissions, waste minimization, biodegradability, and alignment 
with circular economy principles. Finally, we discuss the major challenges and future research directions 
necessary for advancing PCM-based thermal energy storage systems. It is hoped that this article will update 
readers and experts working in this area on the recent advancements in PCM-based TES systems and provide an 
in-depth understanding of ML potentials in revolutionizing PCM-based solar energy storage systems.

1. Introduction
The surge in energy needs to meet explosive population growths, 
technological innovations and economic developments around the 
world has created room for explorations of different energy sources to 
meet the growing demands. Conventional energy generation through 
fossil fuels is widely used globally because of its relatively cheap cost, 
However, these harm the environment in terms of climate change, air 
and water pollution, habitat degradation, acid rain, and ozone layer 
depletion [ 1 , 2 ]. As a result, significant effort has been put into clean and 
renewable energy alternatives such as solar, wind, biomass, geothermal, 
fuel cell, and nuclear energy [ 3 ]. Renewable energy accounted for three- 
fifths of the world's electricity market and two-fifths of the direct fuel 
market by the middle of the 21st century. Global carbon dioxide (CO
2
) 
emissions have been projected to be reduced to 75 % of the 1985 levels 
by 2050 if renewable and energy efficiency are widely employed. In 
addition, the attributed benefit is expected to make it competitive with 
traditional energy [ 4 ]. Although the global COVID-19 pandemic was 
attributed to the slight decrease (3.3 % or 450 Mt) in CO
2 
emissions in 
2020, it goes without saying that the increase in renewable energy's 
share of global electricity was still the key factor in slashing the overall 
emissions.
Solar and wind energy are the most widely used of the available 
clean energy sources due to their availability throughout the year. The 
former has been adjudged as the most efficient energy source due to its 
inexhaustible environmentally benign properties. Solar energy systems 
are well-researched to improve performance and efficiency and reduce 
per-unit energy costs [ 5–7 ]. The fluctuation in the solar energy supply 
due to climatic conditions highlighted the need to develop a novel ma -
terial for the successful utilization and storage of harvested energy from 
solar radiation. Sometimes hybrid energy system is deployed to address 
the intermittency of renewable energy sources. However, such a system 
overly depends on utilizing an auxiliary heater as the secondary source 
of energy. This dependency can be mitigated by the introduction of 
thermal energy storage (TES) towards achieving a cleaner and more 
efficient energy system [ 8 , 9 ]. The TES systems can be used to store 
excess energy for future use when production exceeds demand. These 
systems consist of three main parts: a storage medium, a containment 
system, and energy transfer mechanisms. The storage medium can uti -
lize sensible heat, latent heat (from fusion or vaporization), or a com -
bination of both. The containment system's role is to prevent heat loss by 
enclosing the other components and providing insulation from the

for adding or removing heat from the storage medium [ 10 ]. An effective 
TES system should be able to charge and discharge rapidly while of -

economical. A TES system's success largely depends on its storage ma -
terials' properties, which should have high energy storage capacity, high 
density, good thermal conductivity, minimal volumetric expansion, and 
reliability. Materials meeting these criteria are often referred to as phase 
change materials (PCMs). PCMs store large amounts of energy during 
phase transitions and can release this energy during periods of high 
demand.
Over the years, Thermal energy storage has been carried out in latent 
heat using phase change materials such as hydrated salts, paraffins, etc. 
Phase change materials (PCMs) are widely used in various industries 
such as medicine, telecommunications, textiles, and satellites due to 
their thermo-physical properties. Its implementation in solar thermal 
applications helps to mitigate solar energy intermittency problems and 
improves the daily TES efficiency of a solar plant. These materials are 
essential for improved economies and reduced environmental pollution 
and their utilization is considered an innovative approach to produce a 
continuous power supply. A different class of PCMs available includes 
organic, inorganic, and eutectics, all having a wide range of freezing/ 
melting points (summarized in Fig. 1 ). Choices of these emerging ma -
terials depend on the targeted applications as PCMs exhibit different 
properties such as physical, kinetic, thermodynamic, chemical and 
physicochemical properties as well as economic viability.
The PCMs are used for latent energy storage applications by 
exploiting their capability of storing or releasing large amounts of en -
ergy while keeping the temperature nearly constant. Unlike conven -
tional materials that experience temperature rise upon heating, PCMs 
store a lot of energy without temperature change if heated at a phase 
change temperature. As a result, the most significant property of PCMs is 
the thermal energy storage capacity. In addition, PCM absorbs signifi -
cant energy during a phase transitioning condition and makes it avail -
able when the energy is needed in large quantities, coupled with the 
intermittent solar energy supply influenced by weather conditions 
[ 11 , 12 ]. This attribute of PCMs made them desirable for energy storage 
applications to ensure a reliable and consistent energy supply. Solar 
energy harvesting devices such as concentrated solar power (CSP) sys -
tems use PCMs for energy applications. In a typical device, mirrors or 
lenses are used to focus solar radiation from sunlight onto a receiver, 
which facilitates the heating of working fluid that accelerates the turbine 
to generate electricity. The heat generated during the daytime is stored 
by the PCMs, and released at night or during mild sunlight conditions for 
continuous electricity generation [ 13 , 14 ].
Given the intricacies and difficulties in predicting weather condi -
tions and when energy stress might arise, there is a need to have an 
efficient thermal energy storage system. Batteries and PCMs are used in 
harvesting devices. However, the storage capacity of the batteries is very 
limited, and experts are looking at PCMs as alternatives and significant 
efforts are being made to address some challenges associated with their 
usage in solar energy storage. Appropriate PCM materials are carefully 
selected for the intended applications to avoid some expensive PCMs 
and the propensity of degradation and leakage over time. In additions, 
since the potential of PCMs is activated when there is phase change, and 
it exhibit very low thermal conductivity compared to other materials if it 
does not undergo phase change. The thermal conductivity of PCMs af -
fects their charging and discharging rates with lower charging/dis -
charging time recorded in those with higher thermal conductivities. It is 
important to note that changing thermal conductivity might not neces -
sarily translate to improvement in energy stored or released from the 
phase change materials. However, an improvement in thermal conduc -
tivity has been beneficial in many cases. The advent of nanotechnology 
through the introduction of nanoparticles that have a very high surface- 
to-volume ratio exhibit a remarkable property. For example. Graphene 
and MWCNT nanoparticles have thermal conductivities of about 5300 
W/m.K and 3000 W/m.K, respectively [ 15 , 16 ], compared to that of 
paraffins which is about 0.2 W/m.K [ 17 ]. Hence, the use of nano -
particles with high thermal conductivities in PCMs will improve their 
thermal properties. It has been implemented in some literature and 
showed improved performance over pristine PCMs. Depending on the 
peculiarities, nano-enhanced phase change materials (nano-PCMs) are

tainability aspects of PCM-based solar energy storage. Lastly, the paper 
identifies major challenges and future research directions for advancing

distinguishes itself by providing a holistic view of recent advancements 
in PCM-based TES systems, integrating AI applications, offering quan -
titative insights into nano-PCM performance, and addressing crucial

2. Phase change materials
The PCMs are substances commonly used for storing latent heat 
energy by absorbing and releasing large thermal energy when under -
going a phase change at almost constant temperature. They are used in 
numerous applications, such as thermal energy storage, Temperature 
regulation, HVAC, passive cooling, and heating, and building insulation. 
PCMs are also known as materials with solid-liquid transition phases 
employed for energy storage applications [ 24 ]. Their usage in numerous 
areas is attributed to their distinctive characteristics such as non- 
corrosiveness, low cost, thermal stability, high heat capacity, reli -
ability, high density, less toxic, consistent melting capacity, no super- 
cooling, and non-segregation. PCMs can be classified based on the 
phase change mechanism as shown in Fig. 1 . It can first be categorized 
into Solid-liquid, Liquid-gas, solid-gas, and solid-solid phase trans -
formation. The solid-liquid PCMs are sub-classified into Organic, Inor -
ganic, and Eutectic [ 25 , 26 ] while Polymer belongs to the categories of 
Solid-Solid phase [ 27 – 31 ]. Organic PCMs are extracted from hydrocar -
bons such as paraffin wax or fatty acids. These are cost-effective and 
high energy density and can be used in a wide temperature range 
(15 – 45 
◦
C) [ 32 ] making them suitable for numerous purposes. However, 
their low thermal conductivity limits their implementation in high- 
temperature applications. Unlike organic PCMs, inorganic PCMs are 
suitable for high-temperature applications such as solar thermal energy 
storage. They are typically salts, salt-hydrates, and metals. The most 
appropriate PCMs among metallic salts are chlorides, fluorides, car -
bonates, sulfates, nitrates, sodium sulfate decahydrate, and magnesium 
chloride hexahydrate. Aluminum alloys employed as PCMs exhibit high 
thermal reliability for energy storage applications [ 33 ]. In addition, 
inorganic PCMs possess twice the heat transfer capacity per volume 
compared with organic materials and have a lower cost. However, they 
are corrosive to metal and might reduce the life span of the system. 
Inorganic PCMs have been very promising and have shown numerous 
benefits. However, their commercialization still faces many problems 
such as supercooling, corrosion, and high cost. Eutectic PCMs are a 
combination of Organic and Inorganic PCMs that show congruent 
freezing and melting without segregation. They provide minimal 
corrosion and great thermal conductivity in unison. Typically, eutectic 
PCMs are divided into three categories: inorganic-inorganic, inorganic- 
inorganic, organic-organic, and [ 34 – 38 ]. Binary. Ternary. Quaternary 
and quinary eutectic PCMs have been well studied and reported in the 
literature [ 33 ].
The selection of PCMs for various applications depends on numerous 
factors such as toxicity and environmental impact, chemical stability, 
thermal conductivity, corrosion resistance, compatibility with materials 
and systems, melting point, and latent heat. By choosing the appropriate 
PCM for a given application, it is possible to achieve significant en -
hancements in energy efficiency, cost savings, and environmental sus -
tainability. To determine the effectiveness of PCMs in TES applications, 
their thermophysical properties must be evaluated. These characteristics 
include melting point, density, thermal conductivity, and volumetric 
latent heat storage capacity. When choosing a type of PCM for a

is another critical factor, as higher conductivity enables faster charging 
and discharging rates. If a PCM has low thermal conductivity, en -
hancements such as adding nanoparticles, metal foams, or finned heat 
exchangers should be considered. Similarly, density and volumetric 
energy density are important for applications where space is limited, as 
higher values help minimize the storage system's size. From a chemical 
perspective, thermal stability is essential to ensure that the material 
retains its properties over multiple heating and cooling cycles. The PCM 
must also be compatible with container materials and heat transfer 
fluids to avoid adverse reactions, such as corrosion in systems using salts 
[ 27 ]. Furthermore, non-toxic and biodegradable materials are preferred 
for environmentally friendly applications, emphasizing the importance 
of toxicity and environmental impact. Economic factors like cost- 
effectiveness and availability play a significant role in selecting a 
PCM, especially for large-scale applications. While inorganic salts are 
generally less expensive than organic PCMs, affordability should be 
balanced with performance and operational suitability. Operational 
characteristics such as supercooling and volumetric expansion also in -
fluence PCM selection. Materials should exhibit minimal supercooling to 
ensure reliable phase transitions, which may necessitate the use of ad -
ditives or nucleating agents. Additionally, PCMs with low volumetric 
expansion reduce stress on the containment system during phase 
transitions.
To guide decision-making, a structured framework can be followed. 
The first step is defining the application's temperature range, storage 
capacity, and cycling frequency, alongside constraints like cost and 
environmental impact. Next, PCM candidates are shortlisted based on 
these criteria, followed by an evaluation of trade-offs between thermal 
performance, cost, and operational challenges. Prototyping and testing 
the selected materials under simulated conditions allow for the assess -
ment of real-world performance. Finally, optimization techniques, such 
as machine learning, can be applied to fine-tune the system design 
before large-scale implementation. For specific solar energy applica -
tions, paraffin waxes or fatty acids are suitable for low-temperature solar 
water heating, while eutectic mixtures or salt hydrates are preferred for 
medium-temperature solar air heating. For high-temperature concen -
trated solar power systems, sodium nitrate or eutectic nitrate mixtures 
provide thermal stability and cost-effectiveness. This comprehensive 
approach ensures the PCM selection is aligned with the system's oper -
ational, economic, and environmental requirements.
Recyclability, material compatibility, and economic effectiveness are 
further requirements. The selection of a specific PCM necessitates 
compromises and trade-offs because it is not practical to consider all the 
physical and chemical properties at once. When it comes to uniform 
freezing (i.e., without the need for major supercooling), organic PCMs 
often offer advantages, and they frequently melt uniformly. However, 
PCMs often have low thermal conductivity and volumetric latent heat 
storage capacity, requiring large quantities for practical use, which can 
offset their cost advantages. Inorganic PCMs have higher thermal con -
ductivity and volumetric latent heat storage capacity than organic PCMs 
and are inexpensive and readily available. However, they have draw -
backs such as greater volumetric changes during phase transitions, 
corrosiveness, and supercooling issues. This TES platform's overall 
power rating, reliability, and net efficiency are all harmed by the 
requirement for supercooling. Impurities, surface quality, and 
experimental circumstances all have a big impact on how much super -
cooling occurs.
3. PCMs in solar energy storage
With numerous significant breakthroughs in research and materials 
development, PCM applications have been extended to areas that are not 
captured initially such as passive or active cooling/heating for cascading 
thermal energy systems, smart thermal grids, smart textile materials, 
electronic temperature management, and thermal regulation of the 
batteries [ 39 , 40 ]. The latent energy change in materials influences the 
PCM thermal energy storage (TES) in a concentrated solar thermal 
power plant. It is important to note that PCM storage works in a passive 
manner, i.e., stationary during charge and discharge. Heat transfer into 
PCM is facilitated by the working fluid pumped through a tube registry 
enclosed in the PCM. PCMs find applications in solar water heaters, solar 
greenhouses, solar cooking, solar desalination, solar photovoltaic (PV) 
panels, and solar air heaters. PCM-based latent heat thermal energy 
storage systems can be classified based on the intended applications into 
low, medium, and high temperatures. Low-temperature types can be 
integrated with solar water heating (SWH) systems in either the solar 
collector or water tank [ 41 ]. Low-temperature types could be integrated 
with the SWH system either in the solar collector or water tank [ 42 ]. 
Fig. 2 illustrates the techniques for integrating PCM-based LHTES with 
SWHs. The system design and components consist of a solar collector, 
PCM storage unit, heat exchanger, storage tank, and circulating system. 
The collector captures solar energy and converts it into heat, while the 
storage unit absorbs and stores heat during the melting process. The heat 
from the PCMs is transferred into the water with the help of an 
exchanger. Pumps are used to circulate water within the system and 
stored in the storage tank.
Numerous approaches have been developed by researchers to revo -
lutionize PCMs and their usage in recent years. However, the most 
readily available PCMs used for numerous applications have a lower 
thermal conductivity, responsible for lengthened melting and solidifi -
cation cycles. Various approaches have been suggested to improve the 
heat transfer of PCM, focusing on materials, devices, and systems. These 
include microencapsulating PCM, adding porous media or nanoparticles 
with high thermal conductivity to PCM, and refining the architectures of 
heat storage devices. For LHTES systems, there are various types of heat 
transfer devices, including as packed beds, shell and tube types, and flat 
plate types. Thermal energy storage devices come in various configu -
rations. The flat plate design has a rectangular shape with heat transfer 
pipes embedded within, surrounded by the PCMs. In packed bed sys -
tems, a large number of PCM-containing units fill the space, with the 
heat transfer fluid (HTF) flowing through the gaps to exchange heat. 
Shell and tube designs have three main variations based on pipe 
arrangement: single-pipe, multi-pipe, and triplex tube structures. These 
systems are further classified by how the HTF and PCM are positioned. 
In one configuration, the PCM occupies the shell area while the HTF 
flows through the tubes for heat exchange. Alternatively, the HTF may 
circulate in the outer shell, with the PCM contained inside the inner 
tubes. Each design aims to optimize heat transfer between the HTF and 
PCM for efficient energy storage and release. Currently, the shell and 
tube form is widely utilized in LHTES applications.
Enhancing heat transfer in latent heat thermal energy storage 
(LHTES) devices is effectively achieved by incorporating fin structures, 
which increase the available surface area. This approach is commonly 
used in shell and tube LHTES systems, where phase change material 
(PCM) fills the shell side. The heat transfer fluid (HTF) - typically water 
for low temperatures, or oil/air for medium/high temperatures - flows 
through the tube, exchanging heat with the PCM. The PCM, though 
mostly stationary, experiences slight natural convection when liquid. 
The greatest thermal resistance occurs between the tube and PCM, pri -

Incorporating porous metal structures into the PCMs has been 
identified as an effective method for enhancing thermal performance, 
due to the high thermal conductivity and specific surface area of these 
materials [ 82 – 84 ]. Studies by Ferfera and Madani showed that com -
posite materials combining PCMs with porous metal foams exhibited 
thermal conductivities four times higher than pure PCMs [ 85 ]. The 
structure and morphology of the porous matrix significantly influence 
the thermal performance and storage capacity of heat exchangers. 
Research on PCMs combined with intermittent cell porous metal foam 
structures revealed that various porous parameters affect heat transfer 
characteristics differently. For instance, introducing a porous structure 
with 0.9 porosity could reduce solidification time by a factor of three 
[ 86 ]. Studies on PCMs with honeycomb structure porous media as 
thermal conductivity enhancers also showed remarkable improvements 
in thermal performance compared to pure PCMs [ 87 ]. In addition, the 
melting behavior of composite PCMs is influenced by factors such as 
heater size, porosity, temperature stratification, and Darcy and Rayleigh 
numbers [ 88 – 94 ].
Zhang et al. [ 90 ] found that convective heat transfer plays a crucial 
role in improving heat transfer in PCM and porous skeleton structure 
systems, especially in high-porosity structures. Their results showed that 
structure porosity has a greater influence on thermal performance than 
pore density. PCM performance with porous skeleton structures shows 
significant improvements compared to pure PCMs, with greater en -
hancements observed at lower structure porosities and higher heat 
fluxes. This improvement is attributed to strengthened natural convec -
tion due to temperature increases caused by high heat flux. Decreasing 
structure porosity from 0.97 to 0.8 can improve the thermal storage rate 
from 1.1 to 1.9 times compared to pure PCM. Studies on flow and 
geometrical parameters have shown varying effects on PCM perfor -
mance during charging and discharging processes. Research by Hosseini 
et al. [ 95 ] demonstrated that increasing the quantity and height of 
longitudinal fins in LHTES systems led to improved performance due to 
increased heat transfer surface area. Separate numerical studies by 
Almazoozi [ 96 ] and Shahsavar et al. [ 97 ] using the Finite Element 
Method (FEM) also indicated that enhancing circular fins improves 
system efficiency in LHTES. Furthermore, in a quantitative analysis. In 
their study, Yang et al. [ 98 ] conducted a numerical analysis to examine 
the influence of the number of longitudinal fins in horizontal storage on 
the enhancement of LHTES (Latent Heat Thermal Energy Storage) and 
showed a significant 73 % improvement in performance. To improve the 
efficacy of energy storage, researchers have also experimented with 
adjusting the flow parameter and utilizing various fin geometries 
[ 98 – 100 ]. Therefore, by modifying the geometric flow, and LHTES pa -
rameters of the fins, the efficiency of this system might be enhanced.

4. Machine learning in PCM-based thermal energy storage 
systems
The PCMs are promising substances with high latent heat, useful for 
heating and cooling in different thermal energy storage systems at 
almost a constant temperature. The idea of Integrating TES with AI is 
becoming more and more useful, effective, promising, and vital as AI 
techniques progress at a dizzying rate. Newer generation district energy 
networks rely heavily on TES to connect and intelligently manage many 
types of energy sources and customers for greater economic and envi -
ronmental benefits [ 145 ]. Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to the ca -
pacity of computers or machines to do similar cognitive tasks as humans 
[ 146 ]. Artificial neural networks (ANNs), fuzzy logics (FLs), particle 
swarm optimization (PSOs), and ant colony optimization (ACOs) are just 
a few of the well-known AI methods that draw inspiration from human 
cognitive processes, animal behavior, or natural phenomena, respec -
tively. Predicting the performance characteristics, code optimization, 
and managing operations are some of the areas where AI is used. The AI 
differs from traditional computing (hard computing, HC) in that it em -
ploys approximate models that, through approximate reasoning or 
modeling, can develop durable solutions. AI is a viable alternative for 
dealing with complicated real-world situations [ 147 ] because it can 
accept ambiguity, imprecision, and partial truth even while it is unlikely 
to deliver the absolute optimal or accurate outcomes in perfect agree -
ment with the real equivalents. Artificial intelligence offers a time- 
efficient alternative to PBM simulations like CFD since they demand 
fewer processing resources. The use of Machine Learning (ML) in the 
PCM coupled with the TES has become a major interest for accurately 
predicting and optimizing complex datasets of PCM usage in TES sys -
tems. Machine learning has become popular recently due to the broad 
application of artificial intelligence. Machine Learning can be catego -
rized into supervised learning and unsupervised learning. The former 
uses created models to make reasonable predictions based on evidence 
(known input and output) in the presence of uncertainty. While the 
latter employs the elimination of datasets with input without appro -
priate labelled responses ( Fig. 10 ). Different approaches of Machine 
Learning techniques have been applied, including Artificial Neural 
Networks (ANN), Deep Learning, Clustering, Classification, Regression, 
Ensemble Methods, etc.

Model accuracy can be optimized when large volumes of data are

In general, findings from the survey articles revealed that the ML

5. Nanotechnology-based PCMs
Nanotechnology has transformed the study of the PCMs, notably 
through the inclusion of nanoparticles, resulting in a new class of ma -
terials known as nano-PCMs. While classic PCMs have attractive prop -
erties including low vapor pressure, high latent heat, chemical inertness, 
and stability, they frequently lack the critical attribute of high thermal 
conductivity, which is necessary for effective heat transfer. Nano -
particles with outstanding attributes such as a high surface area to 
volume ratio are cleverly integrated into the PCM matrix to produce 
nano-PCMs with improved thermal properties and performance [ 196 ]. 
Nano-PCMs are composite materials made up of a PCM matrix and 
nanoparticles. These nanoparticles are extremely small, typically 
ranging from 1 to 100 nm in size, and their presence improves the 
thermal properties of the PCM, such as thermal conductivity, specific 
heat capacity, and phase change temperature. Nanoparticles improve 
heat transfer by increasing the surface area of the PCM. Some re -
searchers have demonstrated that the inclusion of nanoparticles in -
creases both the thermal capacity and the thermal conductivity of the 
PCM [ 197 , 198 ].
The use of nanotechnology, including nanoparticles, has assisted the 
development of innovative PCMs with improved properties such as 
increased thermal stability and durability. This improvement improves 
the PCM matrix's efficiency, allowing for greater thermal energy storage 
and release. As a result, nanotechnology has the potential to transform 
thermal energy storage and heat transfer applications, providing more 
efficient and sustainable solutions across a wide range of industries. 
Furthermore, the use of nanotechnology in PCMs increases the PCM's 
lifespan by reducing the danger of degradation caused by frequent phase 
change cycles. Furthermore, it minimizes the amount of PCM required 
for a given application, optimizing cost-effectiveness.
The substantial improvements and effects observed in nano-PCMs 
extend beyond thermal attributes such as latent heat capacity, phase 
change temperature, and PCM durability. Other parameters, like as 
viscosity, subcooling, and density, are altered by the presence of nano -
particles [ 199 ]. Specific nanoparticles also transfer their specific fea -
tures, such as electrical conductivity, insulation, or antibacterial effects, 
into the nano-PCM, allowing them to be used in domains that require 
these properties. Incorporating metal-based nanoparticles such as cop -
per and silver, for example, produces PCMs with antibacterial proper -
ties, which have valuable uses in healthcare [ 200 , 201 ]. Similarly, using 
carbon-based nanoparticles such as graphene and carbon nanotubes 
improves the electrical conductivity of PCMs, making them perfect for 
use in the electronics sector and sensor applications [ 202 , 203 ].
Notably, various nanoparticles used in nano-PCMs include inorganic 
materials such as metals and metal oxides, as well as carbon-based 
materials such as graphene and carbon nanotubes, as illustrated in 
Fig. 17 . Various researchers have studied the impact of nanoparticles on 
PCM properties, including latent heat, phase change temperature, and 
heat storage capacity. For example, Chieruzzi et al. [ 204 ] developed 
nano-PCMs by adding different nanoparticles to a NaNO

5.1. Nano-PCMs in solar energy storage
Nano-PCMs, or nanomaterial-based phase change materials, hold 
significant promise in addressing the limitations of traditional solar 
energy storage technologies. Solar energy, as a clean and abundant 
renewable source, has the potential to counteract climate change and 
lessen our reliance on fossil fuels. However, because of its intermittent 
nature, it demands efficient and cost-effective energy storage technol -
ogies for continuous power supply [ 238 ]. The TES provides a means to 
capture and store solar energy as heat. This enables the utilization of 
solar energy during periods when sunlight is unavailable, such as at 
night or during cloudy weather [ 239 ]. The TES encompasses three major 
approaches: sensible heat storage, thermo-chemical energy storage, and 
latent heat storage [ 240 ]. Sensible heat storage employs solid, liquid, or 
gas materials to store heat energy at a steady temperature without phase 
change. The energy is kept as internal energy and can be retrieved later 
for heating or electrical generation. Thermo-chemical energy storage 
captures solar energy through reversible chemical reactions. Charging 
initiates a heat-absorbing reaction, and discharge releases the stored 
energy, resulting in high density and on-demand release. Latent heat 
storage, the most researched TES method, stores more energy than 
sensible heat storage. It involves phase change at a constant tempera -
ture, providing high energy density. It is useful for high-capacity storage 
in limited space [ 241 ].
However, the traditional phase change material employed in solar 
energy storage technologies has limits in terms of energy density, ther -
mal stability, and overall system performance. This is where nano-PCMs 
come in, harnessing the unique features of nanomaterials. Nano-PCMs 
provide a potential alternative by increasing thermal energy storage 
capacity and improving heat transmission qualities. Their implementa -
tion into solar energy storage systems could overcome current con -
straints and pave the way for more efficient and effective solar power 
consumption. Incorporation of nanoparticles in phase change materials 
has shown a significant improvement and benefit for the LHTES. Several 
efforts have shifted towards introduction of novel PCMs and leakproof 
composite PCMs owing to their excellent thermal durability desirable for 
TES components with energy storage and release capacity [ 242 , 243 ]. 
Organic PCMs such as fatty acids perform better for solar passive LHTES 
due to their desirability and availability. However, the identified setback 
in these materials is chemical interaction with the atmosphere resulting 
in leakages during the transition from solid to liquid state. PCM en -
capsulations have been identified as a possible solution, but they are 
expensive and laborious. Therefore, doping with materials with highly 
thermal conductive materials will go a long way in improving its ther -
mal conductivities. Carbon-based materials such as graphene oxide 
[ 244 ], carbon nanofibers (CNFs) [ 245 – 247 ], expanded graphene [ 248 ], 
MXenes and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) [ 249 , 250 ], and graphene nano -
platelets (GNPs) [ 251 ] have been primarily used to enhance the thermal 
conductivity of organic PCMs. As part of effort to increase the thermal 
conductivity of methyl palmitate organic PCMs by addressing amelio -
rating the leakages problem and poor thermal conductivity issue 
without reducing its LHTES capabilities.

6.1. Economic assessment of PCMS in thermal energy storage
At present, there exist three distinct TES technologies at varying 
stages of development ( Fig. 21 ) : Sensible Heat Storage (SHS), THS, and 
the LHTES [ 239 ]. As for the latter, energy is stored through the phase 
change of the storage medium. During this phase transition, energy is 
released at a constant temperature with high energy density, resulting in 
a system with increased efficiency and reduced thermal losses. PCMs 
serve as the storage media for LHTES due to their significant latent heat 
of fusion, enabling them to store substantial amounts of heat when 
transitioning between phases. However, the low thermal conductivity of 
these PCMs can slow down the charging and discharging processes 
within the TES system, unless additional highly conductive materials 
like graphite powder are incorporated [ 280 ]. Accessing the economic 
impact of PCMs in TES would help to shed light on the economic 
feasibility such as cost analysis and payback period (long-term economic 
benefits) of PCM technology. Moreover, it will provide researchers, 
policymakers, and stakeholders with a comprehensive understanding of 
the transformative potential of PCM-based TES solutions in addressing 
the interlinked challenges of energy security and environmental 
sustainability.
6.1.1. Cost analysis of PCM materials: Raw material, production, and 
manufacturing
The cost analysis of PCMs involves a comprehensive assessment of 
various components, starting with raw material costs. The selection of 
PCM raw materials plays a significant role in determining the overall 
expenses and performance of the TES system. The availability, sourcing 
location, and extraction processes influence the prices of these materials. 
Some PCMs are derived from bio-based (e.g., palm oil, palm kernel oil, 
soybean oil, etc.) or renewable sources [ 283 ], potentially reducing 
material costs and enhancing sustainability. Aridi et al. did a detailed 
comparison between the economic and environmental impacts of four 
PCMs of different types comprising salt hydrates, paraffin, bio-based 
(coconut oil), and biofertilizers (coconut oil using biofertilizers). Their 
findings show that paraffin which is the most expensive also has the 
greatest environmental impact owing to its high latent heat of fusion 
[ 284 ]. Moreover, coconut oil biofertilizers are relatively cheap and the 
most eco-friendly of all. Understanding the price dynamics and potential 
fluctuations in raw material costs is crucial for establishing cost-effective 
PCM production.
Production costs constitute another critical aspect of the cost analysis 
[ 285 ]. These expenses encompass the entire process of transforming raw 
materials into the desired PCM form. Production techniques, such as 
purification, synthesis, and blending processes, contribute to the overall 
costs [ 284 ]. The complexity of these processes and the scale of 
manufacturing operations directly impact the per-unit cost of PCMs 
( Fig. 21 (b)) [ 286 ], making economies of scale an essential consider -
ation. Additionally, research and development investments dedicated to 
improving PCM materials may initially increase manufacturing costs, 
but they hold the promise of driving down expenses over time [ 287 ]. 
Evaluating the energy intensity of production processes is equally vital, 
as it influences both economic factors and the environmental footprint 
of PCM manufacturing. By conducting a thorough cost analysis of PCM 
materials' raw materials, production, and manufacturing, stakeholders 
can better understand the financial feasibility of integrating PCM-based 
thermal energy storage systems into various industrial applications.
The recyclability of PCMs is contingent on the materials used in their 
composition and the encapsulation techniques employed. Some PCMs, 
particularly those derived from bio-based or renewable sources, may 
offer greater potential for recycling, as they can be broken down and 
reprocessed into new PCM products with reduced energy consumption 
and environmental burden [ 288 ]. PCMs with high thermal stability and 
durability can endure numerous charge-discharge cycles without sig -
nificant degradation [ 283 ], enabling their repeated use in various ap -
plications. The ability to reuse PCMs in different systems not only 
reduces the demand for new materials but also mitigates the environ -
mental impact associated with the production and disposal of PCM 
products. Additionally, exploring innovative encapsulation methods 
[ 21 ] that facilitate easy separation of the PCM from the encapsulating 
material can enhance the recyclability and reusability of PCM systems.

6.1.2. Evaluation of long-term economic benefits and potential cost savings
This is crucial to understand the financial viability and attractiveness 
of adopting this technology. PCM-based TES systems offer several ad -
vantages that can translate into substantial cost savings over their 
operational lifetime. PCMs have the potential to store between 5 and 14 
times as much energy in a given volume compared to traditional sensible 
storage materials [ 9 ], as evidenced by a study by Kuznik et al. [ 289 ], 
whereby they found PCMs to store substantial amounts of thermal en -
ergy compared to other materials under the same conditions, Fig. 22 (a). 
This translates to their ability to shift energy consumption to off-peak 
periods when electricity tariffs are typically lower. By charging the 
PCM during periods of excess energy supply and discharging it during 
peak demand hours, users can take advantage of lower electricity rates, 
effectively reducing energy costs.
Particularly, some studies have highlighted the cost-saving potential 
of PCM-based TES systems. For instance, several studies have high -
lighted the cost-saving potential of PCM-based TES systems. Moreover, 
they also show robust quantitative evidence of the impact of PCM-based 
TES systems on improving energy efficiency and enabling effective 
demand-side management (DSM), making them a valuable addition to 
advanced thermal energy systems. For instance, a simulation-based case 
study on PCM systems integrated into residential buildings showed that 
incorporating PCM layers reduces heating and cooling costs by 5 – 20 % 
annually. The initial investment in PCM materials is estimated to be 
offset within 50 years (compared to 70 – 100 years in many other cases) 
due to lower energy consumption, making the system economically 
viable for long-term applications [ 290 ]. In commercial settings, PCM- 
based storage systems have been reported to reduce peak electricity 
demand charges by up to 33 % [ 291 ], providing significant savings for 
facilities with high energy usage. Quantitative assessments indicate that 
the electricity generation levelized cost of storage (LCOS) ranges be -
tween $0.08 and $0.38 per kWh, depending on the PCM material and 
configuration [ 292 ], making them competitive with conventional stor -
age technologies [ 290 , 293 ].
Economic analyses of PCM applications have predominantly relied 
on the simple payback period (PBP) as a key metric. Baniassadi et al. 
[ 294 ] analyzed bio-based PCMs in five Iranian cities to determine the 
economically optimal thickness of PCM. Despite achieving energy sav -
ings of up to 40 % annually, the study concluded that the best economic 
performance was achieved without PCM integration, primarily due to 
the low electricity prices and high initial cost of PCM. These findings 
align with previous observations that the high capital cost of PCMs is a 
major barrier to widespread adoption [ 295 ]. However, bio-based PCMs 
[ 296 ], being among the lowest-cost options available [ 292 ], offer the 
potential for reducing these financial hurdles. Yousefi et al. [ 297 ] 
developed a form-stable composite bio-PCM with a PBP of 25 years in 
Australia, deemed economically feasible due to an assumed building 
lifetime of 50 – 100 years. Similarly, Peippo et al. [ 298 ] evaluated bio- 
PCM fatty acid mixtures in plasterboard applications, achieving PBPs 
of 10 – 20 years based on heating and cooling cost savings. However, 
these analyses often omit reductions in capital costs for heating and 
cooling systems due to reduced capacity requirements. Incorporating 
such savings could further decrease the PBP values. A more compre -
hensive approach was undertaken by Khan et al. [ 299 ], who calculated 
the dynamic payback period (DPBP) of a commercial bio-PCM, ac -
counting for interest rates. While single-story and two-story houses 
showed simple PBPs of 10 – 15 years, the inclusion of DPBP extended the 
PBP to 65 years, emphasizing the significance of a full economic analysis 
that considers financial variables like interest rates. It is vital to note still 
that the PCM integration should be optimized to maximize thermal 
performance under varying conditions. Yildiz et al. [ 300 ] evaluated the 
thermal performance of a water-to-water heat pump (HP) system inte -
grated with a TES unit, designed to store solar energy for use during 
periods of unavailability. Experimental investigations were conducted

6.1.3. Consideration of factors influencing economic viability
The economic viability of PCMs in thermal energy storage hinges on 
a careful consideration of several factors, with market demand being a 
primary determinant. The adoption and acceptance of PCM-based TES 
systems heavily relies on the demand for energy-efficient and sustain -
able solutions across various industries and applications. As seen in 
Fig. 23 [ 303 ], PCMs-based technologies, although still in a small-scale 
level, are completely tested and close to being operational in the 
actual environment. Understanding market trends, consumer prefer -
ences, and regulatory policies is crucial in assessing the potential uptake 
of PCM technology. Market demand not only drives production volumes 
but also influences economies of scale [ 284 ], which can significantly 
impact material costs and, consequently, the overall affordability of 
PCM-based TES systems.
Scalability impacts production costs [ 304 ], as larger quantities often 
enable manufacturers to optimize processes and reduce expenses. 
Moreover, the scalability of PCM-based TES systems also presents their 
flexibility and adaptability across different applications and settings 
[ 305 – 307 ]. Cost-effective integration with existing energy systems and 
technologies is a critical consideration in determining the overall eco -
nomic viability of PCM-based TES. Seamless integration into buildings, 
industrial processes, or renewable energy systems ensures maximum 
efficiency and performance, avoiding the need for costly modifications 
or additional equipment. A successful integration strategy requires 
evaluating the compatibility of PCM systems with other components, as 
well as the potential benefits in terms of energy savings, reduced oper -
ational costs, and overall system performance. Sardari et al. investigated 
the effect of heat storage size in a porous medium for domestic use, 
employing both pure PCM and PCM composite embedded in a copper 
foam. Moreover, their work shows the importance of the surface/vol -
ume size of the PCM involved and the dimensions of the system (rect -
angular 2D system) [ 308 , 309 ]. A flat plate collector whereby the PCM is 
cylindrically encapsulated as reported by Li et al. also improved the 
thermal conductivity across a uniform mean temperature during the 
charging process by integrating erythritol into expanded graphite 
(composite PCM) [ 310 ].

6.2.1. Life cycle assessment of PCMs: Analyzing environmental impacts 
from material extraction to end-of-life
LCA of PCMs offers a comprehensive and holistic approach to un -
derstanding the environmental impact associated with their complete 
life cycle [ 338 ]. This assessment considers the environmental implica -
tions from the initial stages of raw material extraction, through 
manufacturing processes, application in thermal energy storage systems, 
and ultimately, their end-of-life disposition [ 285 ], Fig. 25 , known as 
cradle-to-grave. By examining each stage, LCA provides valuable in -
sights into the potential environmental hotspots and opportunities for 
improving the overall sustainability of PCM technology.
The LCA of PCMs begins with an analysis of raw material extraction, 
encompassing the resources and energy inputs involved in sourcing the 
materials used to produce PCMs. Evaluating the environmental impact 
of raw material extraction sheds light on potential ecological conse -
quences, such as habitat destruction, water pollution, and greenhouse 
gas emissions. In a study conducted by Jungbluth in Germany, an LCA 
was carried out specifically on the production phase of sodium acetate 
(PCM) and expanded for energy storage. It focused exclusively on 
assessing the global warming potential and cumulative energy demand 
associated with sodium acetate. The results indicated that the total 
greenhouse gas emissions resulting from PCM production amounted to 
approximately 5 kg CO
2 
eq. per kilogram of sodium acetate produced. 
Additionally, the study concluded that the incineration of PCM and the 
electricity consumption during its production were the primary envi -
ronmental areas of concern in terms of global warming potential [ 339 ]. 
Horn et al. evaluated the environmental performance of various inno -
vative materials used for energy storage. They specifically analyzed the 
storage cycles for PCMs and thermochemical materials (TCM). The re -
sults showed that, in the case of salt hydrates, it took approximately 20 
to 150 cycles to offset the non-renewable primary energy demand, while 
paraffins required up to 280 cycles for amortization. For thermochem -
ical materials like silica gel and zeolite 13 × , amortization was achieved 
after 60 and 260 cycles, respectively [ 340 ]. Furthermore, in another 
study [ 341 ], the environmental impact of PCM and TCM materials at the 
materials and components level was examined. It was concluded that 
using PCMs, especially in applications with a small temperature differ -
ence such as cooling, could be environmentally beneficial.
Subsequently, the manufacturing phase considers the energy con -
sumption and emissions associated with PCM production processes. This 
stage is crucial in identifying energy-intensive processes and emissions 
of pollutants that could be mitigated through process optimization and 
cleaner production methods. As PCMs are integrated into thermal en -
ergy storage systems, their operational impact, including energy effi -
ciency and potential greenhouse gas emissions reduction, is assessed. 
No ¨el et al. conducted an LCA on two biologically sourced PCMs 
(Dodecanoic acid: sourced from palm kernel oil, and ethyl hex -
adecanoate: sourced from algae), used for energy storage, specifically 
concentrating on the energy consumed and CO

emissions. The findings

16 metric tons of CO
2 
equivalent over 10 years in a typical house in the 
USA [ 342 ]. Karasu et al., conducted research in the Drake Landing house

6.3. Comparison of PCM-based TES with conventional energy storage in 
terms of economic and environmental footprint
When comparing PCM costs with conventional energy storage 
methods like batteries and pumped hydro, several key factors come into 
play, impacting the economic viability and suitability for different ap -
plications. PCMs offer distinct advantages in terms of material costs 
compared to conventional energy storage methods [ 286 , 301 ]. While 
batteries, especially lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), have seen significant 
cost reductions in recent years, they still involve expensive raw mate -
rials [ 348 ], such as lithium and cobalt, which can fluctuate due to supply 
chain dynamics. On the other hand, PCMs utilize a range of materials, 
some of which can be derived from renewable sources [ 301 ], potentially 
reducing overall material costs and mitigating environmental concerns. 
Additionally, PCM systems often have a longer operational life and can 
endure numerous charge-discharge cycles without substantial degrada -
tion, contributing to cost-effectiveness over time [ 287 ]. In contrast, 
batteries typically have a finite lifespan and require eventual replace -
ment, adding to their lifecycle costs. Sachin et al. reported the potential 
use of PCM-based energy storage systems in hybrid and electric vehicles 
that rely on batteries. Given that batteries tend to function inadequately 
in cold temperatures, employing a PCM-based system to swiftly warm 
the batteries could help mitigate the decline in battery performance 
during chilly weather conditions [ 349 ].
Furthermore, the capital investment required for setting up PCM- 
based TES systems is often more manageable compared to the massive 
infrastructure needed for pumped hydro installations. Pumped hydro 
facilities demand large-scale reservoirs, sophisticated pumping mecha -
nisms, and substantial construction expenses, which can lead to higher 
upfront costs. Moreover, batteries or pumped hydro facilities cannot on 
their own store thermal energy. PCM systems, depending on the appli -
cation, may necessitate less-complex infrastructures, reducing the initial 
investment required. While conventional energy storage methods like 
batteries and pumped hydro continue to dominate various energy stor -
age markets, PCM technology presents a compelling alternative, 
particularly for certain applications and scenarios [ 350 ].
Batteries are recognized for their susceptibility to thermal risks [ 351 ] 
(low-temperature region: the flow of electrolyte decreases which de -
creases performance, high-temperature region: thermal runaway which 
reduces battery's lifespan) [ 352 – 354 ], and PCMs are frequently 
employed for effective thermal regulation, Fig. 27 a, [ 355 – 357 ], partic -
ularly in the case of LIBs. Heyhat et al. conducted an assessment on the 
thermal efficiency of a passive thermal management system (TMS) 
applied to an 18,650 LIB, Fig. 27 c, incorporating PCMs, Fig. 25 d, porous 
metal foam, fins, and nanoparticles [ 358 ]. They observed that the PCM- 
incorporated system has the best battery mean temperature. Moreover, a 
porous-PCM composite even showed a 4 to 6 K temperature reduction, 
Fig. 27 b, although with an observed delay in the melting initiation time 
(MIT) which may pose operational challenges on the battery. Huang and 
colleagues created a composite PCM using paraffin, ethylene glycol 
(EG), and epoxy resin. They also devised a Battery Thermal Management 
(BTM) system that incorporates this PCM along with a heat pipe. This 
BTM system effectively maintains the battery temperature below 44 
◦
C, 
ensuring a minimal temperature fluctuation and extended operational 
duration [ 359 ]. In the study by Liu and co-researchers, they developed 
composite PCMs comprising paraffin, nano ‑ silver, EG, and high-density 
polyethylene. These PCMs exhibited minimal leakage, high latent heat 
capacity, and excellent thermal conductivity. Additionally, they inte -
grated this PCM-based the BTM system with liquid cooling. When sub -
jected to a 3C discharging and 1C charging scenario, the battery's peak 
temperature reached 59.5 
◦
C, with a temperature difference of 6.1 
◦
C. 
This performance was superior to that achieved with a single liquid 
cooling system [ 360 ].
It is important to note that the utilization of PCMs in BTM systems 
(TMSs) comes with several drawbacks. These include limited heat ab -
sorption capacity, a substantial increase in battery temperature once the 
PCM has fully melted, and the need for a re-preparation process. 
Furthermore, using PCM in cold weather can reduce the rate at which 
the battery warms up during initial driving, potentially harming the 
battery [ 361 ]. Furthermore, in BTM, the primary emphasis of PCM 
research has been on organic PCMs like paraffin and PEG, which possess 
inherent flammability. Such organic PCMs can become combustible 
materials, potentially exacerbating the spread of thermal runaway 
within LIBs and leading to incidents such as fires and explosions. To 
address these unfavorable aspects, inorganic PCMs should be considered 
in BTM owing to their flame-retardant properties and electrical insu -
lation ability [ 362 ]. Moreover, researchers have proposed a combina -
tion of passive and active approaches, such as the simultaneous use of 
PCM with airflow or PCM with liquids. In such hybrid TMSs, the pres -
ence of PCM helps delay the rise in battery temperature and the acti -
vation of the active cooling system [ 363 ], resulting in significant cost 
savings for the energy storage companies.

6.4. Overview of challenges and potential strategies to optimize economic 
and environmental performances
The implementation of PCM-based TES systems comes with its share 
of challenges, which must be addressed to optimize both economic and 
environmental performance. One significant challenge lies in the se -
lection of suitable PCM materials that strike a balance between thermal 
properties, cost-effectiveness, and environmental impact [ 362 , 364 ]. 
Research and development efforts must focus on identifying optimal 
PCMs, Fig. 28 , with high energy storage capacities, appropriate phase 
change temperatures, and minimal environmental footprints. Addi -
tionally, advancements in encapsulation technologies are essential to 
enhance the durability and reusability of PCM materials, reducing the 
need for frequent replacements and minimizing waste generation. 
Furthermore, optimizing system design and scaling strategies can lead to 
cost savings and improved efficiency.
Another challenge is the economic feasibility of PCM-based TES 
systems, particularly in comparison to conventional energy storage 
methods. While PCMs offer various environmental benefits, their initial 
costs may be higher than traditional alternatives. To address this, 
financial incentives, government support, and innovative financing 
models can be employed to promote the adoption of PCM technology. 
Furthermore, life cycle cost analysis can aid in identifying long-term 
savings and payback periods, providing a more comprehensive picture 
of the economic benefits. Additionally, strategic deployment of PCM- 
based TES systems in specific applications where energy demand fluc -
tuates significantly can optimize energy consumption and maximize 
cost-effectiveness.

classification based on the variables and research objectives. Also, 
the use of statistical methods with factors requires huge costs for data 
collection and sorting.
8. Conclusions and future directions
This article provides a comprehensive review of recent advance -
ments in the use of phase change materials for thermal energy storage 
systems, highlighting their potential for enhancing heat transfer and 
thermal conductivity through various innovative approaches. Notably, 
the study emphasizes the novelty of integrating nanoparticle dispersion, 
porous metallic foam, cascaded PCMs, extended surfaces, and heat pipes 
in PCM-based systems. The implementation of artificial intelligence in 
optimizing these systems is also discussed, showcasing its predictive 
capabilities. The investigation focused on Melting and solidification 
time, charging and discharging rates, energy cost, exergy efficiency, and 
specific energy consumption, which determines the improvement in 
thermal energy systems based on technical, economic, and environ -
mental considerations. Key findings include: 
• Most of the literature reported that the shapes and fin thickness 
modifications affected the thermal performance of LHTES.
• Thermal cycles have negligible impact on the thermophysical prop -
erties of nano-PCMs and the specific heat capacities, densities, and 
thermal conductivities of solids and liquid PCMs and nano-PCMs are 
not consistent across literatures. As such there is a need to stan -
dardize the test method and come up with a reliable database.
• Virtually, all surveyed articles showed an increase in phase change 
rate with the introduction of nanoparticles, indicating that the 
quantity of energy stored/released during the phase change process 
is improved.
• Novel methods, such as utilizing composite the PCMs and micro -
encapsulated PCMs, have effectively tackled issues such as low 
thermal conductivity, supercooling, and leakage. This has resulted in

based on PCM.
• Applications of nanotechnology through the dispersion of nano -
particles, inserting metal fiber structures, and mixing with graphite 
addressed the low thermal conductivity problem associated with 
some PCMs.
• Selection of suitable PCM for solar energy storage applications de -
pends on numerous parameters including wind velocity, solar radi -
ation intensity, ambient temperature, latitude of location etc.
• Additional studies are required on the heat transfer enhancements of 
PCMs, particularly inorganic PCMs with poor thermal conductivities 
and paucity of studies on their improvement by highly conductive 
nanoparticle dispersion compared to organic PCMs.
• When choosing PCMs for thermal storage, it is crucial to consider 
factors such as melting temperature, sensible/latent heat, and ther -
mal conductivity. While considering market instability, it is impor -
tant to additionally consider costs, albeit their significance may be 
relatively lower. For the development of a more ecologically sus -
tainable thermal storage system, it is crucial to take into account 
economic, safety, and environmental factors. Optimization tools, 
which are a type of multi-criteria decision-making technique, can be 
used for selecting and developing PCM.
• An evaluation of the environmental consequences of PCMs has un -
covered both favorable and unfavorable features, highlighting the 
necessity of creating environmentally friendly and sustainable PCM 
solutions to address potential issues related to toxicity and 
recyclability.
• Utilizing PCMs in solar energy storage systems can effectively miti -
gate greenhouse gas emissions and decrease reliance on fossil fuels, 
hence facilitating the shift towards a more environmentally friendly 
energy framework.
• Advancements in computational modeling and simulation of PCM- 
based thermal energy storage systems can aid in the optimization 
of system design, performance, and integration with solar energy 
applications.
• Of the ML techniques explored in the literature, ANN, ANN offers 
several advantages, including flexibility, and affordable, and reliable 
data processing when compared to traditional modeling techniques 
like CFD. Additionally, ANN proved to be reliable and efficient at 
analyzing diverse heat transfer processes.
• It should be noted that ANN-based predictive control models are

to handle with traditional modeling techniques). Additionally, ANN-

Despite the extensive synthesis of recent advancements in phase 
change materials, the study acknowledges several limitations, including 
the veracity of some reported findings in the surveyed articles, oversight 
on some literature not covered, and scientific interpretations of some 
findings. Future research should prioritize the development of robust 
encapsulation techniques, optimization of manufacturing processes, and 
comprehensive lifecycle assessments of these materials to ensure to 
ensure economic feasibility and sustainability.
